What You Already Know

In Calculus I, a differentiable curve $y = f(x)$ has tangent line $y - y_0 = f'(x_0)(x - x_0)$ using a single slope $f'(x_0)$. In Section 11.5, you saw that any plane through $P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ with normal vector $\mathbf{n} = \langle A, B, C \rangle$ satisfies $\mathbf{n} \cdot \langle x - x_0, y - y_0, z - z_0 \rangle = 0$, and any line through $P_0$ in direction $\mathbf{v}$ uses that same vector for its direction numbers. And in Section 13.6, you proved that the 2D gradient $\nabla f(x_0, y_0)$ is always orthogonal to the level curve $f(x, y) = k$. Section 13.7 lifts that exact orthogonality idea one dimension higher into 3D space.

Core Concept • §1

1. Tangent Plane & Normal Line via $\nabla F(x_0, y_0, z_0)$

Intuition: Imagine resting a rigid flat pane of glass against a smooth sphere or bowl at a single point $P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)$, while driving a straight pin through $P_0$ perpendicular to that glass pane (Textbook p. 931, Billiard Ball Exploration & Figures 13.55–13.56). The flat glass is the tangent plane, and the perpendicular pin (the line of impact) is the normal line. Writing a surface in general 3-variable form $F(x, y, z) = 0$ puts $x$, $y$, and $z$ on equal footing so the 3D gradient $\nabla F(P_0)$ gives that perpendicular direction in one step (Textbook pp. 931–932, Example 1 & Figure 13.57).

Note (Instructor Notes vs. Book): Larson writes gradients with $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$ (Textbook p. 934), whereas Dr. Alhumaidy’s notes (pp. 932, 934) use component brackets $\nabla F(x_0, y_0, z_0) = \langle F_x, F_y, F_z \rangle \neq \langle 0, 0, 0 \rangle$ and solve explicit surfaces directly via $z = f(x_0, y_0) + f_x(x_0, y_0)(x - x_0) + f_y(x_0, y_0)(y - y_0)$ rather than $f_x(x-x_0)+f_y(y-y_0)-(z-z_0)=0$. Both forms describe the exact same plane; follow your instructor’s notation for speed on exams.
Level surface $F(x,y,z)=0$ Tangent plane through $P_0$ Normal vector $\nabla F(P_0)$ & normal line Tangent vectors $\mathbf{r}'(t_0)$ in the plane

Notice that $\nabla F(P_0)$ makes a $90^\circ$ angle with every tangent vector $\mathbf{r}'(t_0)$ lying in the tangent plane at $P_0$ (cf. Textbook p. 932, Figure 13.57).

Formulas (Textbook p. 932, Theorem 13.13 & p. 933): Let $F$ be differentiable at $P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ on $F(x, y, z) = 0$ with $\nabla F(P_0) = \langle F_x(P_0), F_y(P_0), F_z(P_0) \rangle \neq \langle 0, 0, 0 \rangle$.

  • Tangent plane to $F(x, y, z) = 0$: $$F_x(P_0)(x - x_0) + F_y(P_0)(y - y_0) + F_z(P_0)(z - z_0) = 0$$
  • Special explicit case $z = f(x, y)$: Setting $F(x, y, z) = f(x, y) - z = 0$ yields $\nabla F(P_0) = \langle f_x(x_0, y_0), f_y(x_0, y_0), -1 \rangle$, so: $$z = f(x_0, y_0) + f_x(x_0, y_0)(x - x_0) + f_y(x_0, y_0)(y - y_0)$$
  • Normal line at $P_0$: $$\frac{x - x_0}{F_x(P_0)} = \frac{y - y_0}{F_y(P_0)} = \frac{z - z_0}{F_z(P_0)} \quad \text{(provided } F_x, F_y, F_z \neq 0 \text{)}$$

Tiny Example (Textbook p. 937, Ex. 18): Find the tangent plane and symmetric normal line to the sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 9$ at $P_0(1, 2, 2)$.

Set $F(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 9 = 0$. Then $\nabla F = \langle 2x, 2y, 2z \rangle$, so $\nabla F(1, 2, 2) = \langle 2, 4, 4 \rangle$ (parallel to $\mathbf{n}_1 = \langle 1, 2, 2 \rangle$). Thus the tangent plane is $1(x - 1) + 2(y - 2) + 2(z - 2) = 0 \implies x + 2y + 2z = 9$, and the symmetric normal line is $\dfrac{x - 1}{1} = \dfrac{y - 2}{2} = \dfrac{z - 2}{2}$.

Why It Works (Chain Rule Proof, Textbook p. 932 & p. 936 Theorem 13.14): For any differentiable curve $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), z(t) \rangle$ on $S$ passing through $P_0 = \mathbf{r}(t_0)$, we have $F(x(t), y(t), z(t)) = 0$ for all $t$. Differentiating by the Chain Rule gives $\frac{d}{dt}[F(\mathbf{r}(t))] = \nabla F(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t) = 0$. Evaluating at $t = t_0$ shows $\nabla F(P_0) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t_0) = 0$, so $\nabla F(P_0)$ is orthogonal to every tangent vector at $P_0$. Unlike the 2D gradient $\nabla f(x, y)$ (which lives in the $xy$-plane and is normal to level curves), $\nabla F(x, y, z)$ lives in $\mathbb{R}^3$ and is normal to level surfaces (Textbook p. 936).

Quick Check 1: Which vector is normal to the surface $z = x^2 - 3y$ at the point $P_0(2, 1, 1)$?
(A) $\langle 4, -3, 1 \rangle$ (B) $\langle 4, -3, -1 \rangle$ (C) $\langle 4, -3, 0 \rangle$ (D) $\langle 2, -3, -1 \rangle$
Check Answer & Reason
Answer: (B) $\langle 4, -3, -1 \rangle$. Move $z$ to the same side: $F(x, y, z) = x^2 - 3y - z = 0$. Then $\nabla F(x, y, z) = \langle 2x, -3, -1 \rangle$, so $\nabla F(2, 1, 1) = \langle 4, -3, -1 \rangle$.

2. Tangent Line to the Intersection Curve of Two Surfaces

Intuition: When two smooth surfaces $F(x, y, z) = 0$ and $G(x, y, z) = 0$ slice through each other, their seam forms a 3D space curve $C$. At any shared point $P_0$ on $C$, the tangent line to $C$ is simply the line where the two tangent planes intersect — meaning its direction vector $\mathbf{v}$ must stay perpendicular to both $\nabla F(P_0)$ and $\nabla G(P_0)$ at the same time (Textbook p. 935, Figure 13.61; Alhumaidy notes p. 937).

$\nabla F(P_0)$ normal to $F=0$ $\nabla G(P_0)$ normal to $G=0$ Cross product $\mathbf{v} = \nabla F(P_0) \times \nabla G(P_0)$

Because the intersection curve lies on both surfaces simultaneously, its tangent direction $\mathbf{v}$ is orthogonal to both $\nabla F(P_0)$ and $\nabla G(P_0)$, matching their cross product.

Formula (Textbook p. 935): If $\nabla F(P_0) \times \nabla G(P_0) = \langle a, b, c \rangle \neq \langle 0, 0, 0 \rangle$, then parametric equations of the tangent line to the intersection curve at $P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ are:

$$x = x_0 + at, \qquad y = y_0 + bt, \qquad z = z_0 + ct$$

Tiny Example (Textbook p. 935, Example 5): Find parametric equations for the tangent line to the intersection of the ellipsoid $x^2 + 2y^2 + 2z^2 = 20$ and the paraboloid $x^2 + y^2 + z = 4$ at $(0, 1, 3)$.

Set $F = x^2 + 2y^2 + 2z^2 - 20$ and $G = x^2 + y^2 + z - 4$. At $(0, 1, 3)$: $\nabla F(0, 1, 3) = \langle 0, 4, 12 \rangle$ and $\nabla G(0, 1, 3) = \langle 0, 2, 1 \rangle$. Thus: $$\mathbf{v} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 0 & 4 & 12 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = \langle 4(1) - 12(2),\; 0,\; 0 \rangle = \langle -20, 0, 0 \rangle$$ Dividing by $-20$ gives direction numbers $1, 0, 0$, so the tangent line is $x = t,\; y = 1,\; z = 3$.

Why It Works: Any curve $C$ shared by $F = 0$ and $G = 0$ has tangent vector $\mathbf{v}$ at $P_0$ inside both tangent planes, forcing $\mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla F(P_0) = 0$ and $\mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla G(P_0) = 0$. The cross product $\nabla F(P_0) \times \nabla G(P_0)$ produces a vector perpendicular to both normals.

Quick Check 2 (Textbook p. 937, Ex. 27): Which vector is parallel to the tangent line to the intersection curve of $x^2 + y^2 = 2$ and $z = x$ at $(1, 1, 1)$?
(A) $\langle 1, 1, 1 \rangle$ (B) $\langle 1, -1, 1 \rangle$ (C) $\langle 1, -1, 0 \rangle$ (D) $\langle 2, 2, -1 \rangle$
Check Answer & Reason
Answer: (B) $\langle 1, -1, 1 \rangle$. Using $F = x^2 + y^2 - 2$ and $G = x - z$: $\nabla F(1, 1, 1) = \langle 2, 2, 0 \rangle$ and $\nabla G(1, 1, 1) = \langle 1, 0, -1 \rangle$. Their cross product is $\langle -2, 2, -2 \rangle = -2\langle 1, -1, 1 \rangle$.

3. Angle of Inclination of a Tangent Plane

Intuition: The angle of inclination $\theta$ ($0 \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$) of a tangent plane is the acute tilt angle between the tangent plane and the flat horizontal $xy$-plane (Textbook p. 935, Figure 13.62). Imagine resting a flat board on the floor and tilting it up by $\theta$: a perpendicular pole glued straight out of the board tilts away from the vertical vector $\mathbf{k} = \langle 0, 0, 1 \rangle$ by that exact same angle $\theta$.

Tangent plane tilted by $\theta$ Vertical normal $\mathbf{k} = \langle 0,0,1\rangle$ Surface normal $\mathbf{n} = \nabla F(P_0)$ Equal acute angles $\theta \approx 35.3^\circ$

Side cross-section drawn to exact scale for Textbook p. 936 Example 6 ($\theta = \arccos\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}} \approx 35.3^\circ$): tilting the plane by $\theta$ tilts its normal $\mathbf{n}$ away from $\mathbf{k}$ by the same angle $\theta$.

Formulas (Textbook pp. 935–936): Since $\mathbf{k} = \langle 0, 0, 1 \rangle$ has length $\|\mathbf{k}\| = 1$, the acute angle $\theta \in \left[0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$ between a plane with normal $\mathbf{n} = \nabla F(P_0)$ and the $xy$-plane satisfies:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{|\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{k}|}{\|\mathbf{n}\|\,\|\mathbf{k}\|} = \frac{|F_z(P_0)|}{\sqrt{F_x(P_0)^2 + F_y(P_0)^2 + F_z(P_0)^2}} \qquad \text{or for } z = f(x, y): \quad \cos\theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{[f_x(x_0, y_0)]^2 + [f_y(x_0, y_0)]^2 + 1}}$$

Tiny Example (Textbook p. 936, Example 6): Find the angle of inclination of the tangent plane to the ellipsoid $\dfrac{x^2}{12} + \dfrac{y^2}{12} + \dfrac{z^2}{3} = 1$ at $(2, 2, 1)$.

Let $F(x, y, z) = \dfrac{x^2}{12} + \dfrac{y^2}{12} + \dfrac{z^2}{3} - 1$. Then $\nabla F(x, y, z) = \left\langle \dfrac{x}{6},\; \dfrac{y}{6},\; \dfrac{2z}{3} \right\rangle$, so $\nabla F(2, 2, 1) = \left\langle \dfrac{1}{3},\; \dfrac{1}{3},\; \dfrac{2}{3} \right\rangle$. Therefore: $$\cos\theta = \frac{2/3}{\sqrt{(1/3)^2 + (1/3)^2 + (2/3)^2}} = \frac{2/3}{\sqrt{6}/3} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}} \implies \theta = \arccos\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}} \approx 35.3^\circ$$

Why It Works: By Section 11.5, the acute dihedral angle between two planes equals the acute angle between their normal vectors. Absolute value $|\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{k}|$ guarantees $\cos\theta \ge 0$ so $\theta \in \left[0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$ regardless of whether $\nabla F$ points upward or downward (e.g. $F = f(x, y) - z$ vs. $F = z - f(x, y)$).

(A) $\arccos\!\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{21}}\right)$ (B) $\arcsin\!\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{21}}\right)$ (C) $\arccos\!\left(\dfrac{4}{\sqrt{21}}\right)$ (D) $0$

4. Locating Horizontal, Parallel, or Perpendicular Tangent Planes

Intuition: Instead of being handed $P_0$ directly, KFUPM exam problems frequently ask you to find the point $(a, b, c)$ on a surface where its tangent plane satisfies a geometric condition — such as being horizontal, parallel to a given plane, or perpendicular to a given line or second surface (Textbook pp. 937–938, Exercises 37–51; Alhumaidy notes pp. 937–938). Every one of these problems translates into a simple vector equation on $\nabla F(x, y, z)$.

Surface gradient $\nabla F(P_0)$ Target plane normal $\mathbf{n}$ Second surface gradient $\nabla G(P_0)$

Every geometric condition on tangent planes turns into an algebraic condition on $\nabla F(P_0)$ plus substituting back into the surface equation.

Rules & Steps (Alhumaidy notes pp. 937–938):

  • Horizontal tangent plane on $z = f(x, y)$: Set $f_x(x, y) = 0$ and $f_y(x, y) = 0$, solve for $(x_0, y_0)$, then substitute back into $z = f(x_0, y_0)$ to get $z_0$ (Alhumaidy p. 937).
  • Tangent plane parallel to a given plane $Ax + By + Cz = D$ (or perpendicular to a line with direction $\mathbf{v}$): Solve $\nabla F(x_0, y_0, z_0) = \alpha\,\mathbf{n}$ for $x_0, y_0, z_0$ in terms of the scalar $\alpha \neq 0$, then substitute into the surface equation to solve for $\alpha$ (Alhumaidy p. 938, Ex. 49).
  • Tangent surfaces / Perpendicular surfaces at $P_0$: First verify $P_0$ satisfies both surface equations; then two surfaces are tangent if $\nabla F(P_0) \parallel \nabla G(P_0)$ (same tangent plane), and perpendicular if $\nabla F(P_0) \cdot \nabla G(P_0) = 0$ (Textbook p. 938, Ex. 43–46).

Tiny Example (Textbook p. 937, Ex. 37 / Alhumaidy notes): Find the point on $z = 3 - x^2 - y^2 + 6y$ where the tangent plane is horizontal.

Set $f_x = -2x = 0 \implies x = 0$ and $f_y = -2y + 6 = 0 \implies y = 3$. Substituting $(0, 3)$ back into $z$ gives $z = 3 - 0^2 - 3^2 + 6(3) = 12$, so the point is $(0, 3, 12)$.

Quick Check 4 (KFUPM Final 251 Q9): If $(a, b, c)$ with $a < 0$ is the point on $4x^2 + 2y^2 - z^2 = 100$ where the tangent plane is parallel to the $yz$-plane ($x = 0$), what is $a^2 + b^2 + c^2$?
(A) $16$ (B) $25$ (C) $100$ (D) $225$
Check Answer & Reason
Answer: (B) $25$. The $yz$-plane has normal $\langle 1, 0, 0 \rangle$. Since $\nabla F(a, b, c) = \langle 8a, 4b, -2c \rangle = \alpha\langle 1, 0, 0 \rangle$, we get $b = 0$ and $c = 0$. Substituting into the surface gives $4a^2 = 100 \implies a = -5$ (as $a < 0$), so $(-5)^2 + 0 + 0 = 25$.
Master Formulas

Section 13.7 Formula Reference & Edge Cases

Core Equations & Required Conditions ($F$ differentiable at $P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)$, $\nabla F(P_0) \neq \langle 0, 0, 0 \rangle$)

  • Level Surface $F(x, y, z) = 0$ — Tangent Plane: $$F_x(P_0)(x - x_0) + F_y(P_0)(y - y_0) + F_z(P_0)(z - z_0) = 0$$
  • Explicit Surface $z = f(x, y)$ — Tangent Plane: $$z = f(x_0, y_0) + f_x(x_0, y_0)(x - x_0) + f_y(x_0, y_0)(y - y_0)$$
  • Normal Line at $P_0$: $$\begin{aligned} x &= x_0 + F_x(P_0)t, \\ y &= y_0 + F_y(P_0)t, \\ z &= z_0 + F_z(P_0)t \end{aligned} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \frac{x - x_0}{F_x(P_0)} = \frac{y - y_0}{F_y(P_0)} = \frac{z - z_0}{F_z(P_0)}$$ Edge case: If any component vanishes (say $F_x(P_0) = 0$), write $x = x_0$ separately and keep symmetric form only for nonzero denominators, e.g., $x = x_0,\; \frac{y - y_0}{F_y(P_0)} = \frac{z - z_0}{F_z(P_0)}$.
  • Tangent Line to Intersection of $F = 0$ and $G = 0$: Direction $\mathbf{v} = \nabla F(P_0) \times \nabla G(P_0)$ (requires $\mathbf{v} \neq \langle 0, 0, 0 \rangle$; otherwise the surfaces are tangent or fail regularity at $P_0$).
  • Angle of Inclination ($0 \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$): $$\cos\theta = \frac{|F_z(P_0)|}{\|\nabla F(P_0)\|} \qquad \text{or for } z = f(x, y): \quad \cos\theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{[f_x(x_0, y_0)]^2 + [f_y(x_0, y_0)]^2 + 1}}$$ Edge cases: Horizontal plane $\implies \theta = 0$; vertical plane ($F_z(P_0) = 0$) $\implies \theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$.
  • Quadric Tangent Shortcuts (Textbook pp. 938–939, Ex. 57–59): At $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$, tangent planes to $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} \pm \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1$ take the form $\frac{x_0 x}{a^2} + \frac{y_0 y}{b^2} \pm \frac{z_0 z}{c^2} = 1$, and any tangent plane to a cone $z^2 = a^2 x^2 + b^2 y^2$ (away from $(0,0,0)$) passes through the origin $(0,0,0)$.
Exam Traps

5 Classic Mistakes (WRONG vs. RIGHT)

Trap 1 — Wrong sign on $z$ when converting $z = f(x, y)$ to $F(x, y, z) = 0$:

WrongWriting $\nabla F = \langle f_x, f_y, +1 \rangle$ for $z = x^2 + y^2$.
RightMove everything to one side: $F = f(x, y) - z = 0 \implies \nabla F = \langle f_x, f_y, -1 \rangle$.

Trap 2 — Leaving variables $(x, y, z)$ inside the normal coefficients:

Wrong$2x(x - 1) + 4y(y - 2) + 6z(z - 1) = 0$ (this is quadratic, not a plane!).
RightEvaluate $F_x, F_y, F_z$ at $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ first to get numbers before writing the plane.

Trap 3 — Ignoring the fixed coefficient or constant in KFUPM MCQs:

WrongGetting $4x - 4y + 2z + 2 = 0$ and reading $a = 4, b = -4, c = 2$ when the prompt asks for $ax + by + cz + 1 = 0$.
RightDivide the entire equation by $2$ first to match $+1$: $2x - 2y + z + 1 = 0 \implies (a,b,c) = (2,-2,1)$.

Trap 4 — Forgetting scalar $\alpha$ in parallel-plane problems or dividing by zero in symmetric equations:

WrongSetting $\nabla F(a, b, c) = \mathbf{n}$ without a scalar $\alpha$, or writing $\dfrac{z - 3}{0}$ in symmetric equations.
RightAlways solve $\nabla F(a, b, c) = \alpha\,\mathbf{n}$, and write zero-component lines as $z = 3,\; \dfrac{x-x_0}{a} = \dfrac{y-y_0}{b}$.

Trap 5 — Using $\sin\theta$ or forgetting absolute value in angle of inclination:

WrongComputing $\sin\theta = \dfrac{F_z}{\|\nabla F\|}$ or leaving a negative $\cos\theta$ from $F_z = -1$.
RightAngle between two planes uses $\cos\theta = \dfrac{|F_z(P_0)|}{\|\nabla F(P_0)\|} \ge 0$ so $0 \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$.

Tangent Plane & Normal Line to a Hyperboloid

Find an equation of the tangent plane and symmetric equations of the normal line to the hyperboloid $z^2 - 2x^2 - 2y^2 = 12$ at $P_0(1, -1, 4)$.
Final Answer: Tangent plane: $x - y - 2z + 6 = 0$. Normal line: $\dfrac{x - 1}{-1} = \dfrac{y + 1}{1} = \dfrac{z - 4}{2}$.
1
Define $F(x, y, z) = 0$ and Compute $\nabla F$
$$F(x, y, z) = z^2 - 2x^2 - 2y^2 - 12 = 0$$ $$F_x = -4x, \qquad F_y = -4y, \qquad F_z = 2z$$
2
Evaluate $\nabla F$ at $P_0(1, -1, 4)$
$$\nabla F(1, -1, 4) = \langle -4(1),\; -4(-1),\; 2(4) \rangle = \langle -4,\; 4,\; 8 \rangle = 4\langle -1, 1, 2 \rangle$$
3
Write Tangent Plane & Normal Line Equations
$$\begin{aligned} -4(x - 1) + 4(y + 1) + 8(z - 4) &= 0 \\ -4x + 4 + 4y + 4 + 8z - 32 &= 0 \\ -4x + 4y + 8z - 24 &= 0 \\ x - y - 2z + 6 &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

Symmetric normal line using direction $\langle -1, 1, 2 \rangle$:

$$\frac{x - 1}{-1} = \frac{y + 1}{1} = \frac{z - 4}{2}$$
Verification: Both equations contain $P_0(1, -1, 4)$: surface gives $4^2 - 2(1)^2 - 2(-1)^2 = 16 - 2 - 2 = 12$; plane gives $1 - (-1) - 2(4) + 6 = 0$.
Example 2 • Explicit Surface & Coefficient Matching • KFUPM Final Term 253 Q11

Matching Standard Form $ax + by + cz + 1 = 0$

If $ax + by + cz + 1 = 0$ is an equation for the tangent plane to the surface $z = (x - y)^2$ at the point $(1, 2, 1)$, find $a + b + c$.
Final Answer: $a + b + c = 1$ (from $2x - 2y + z + 1 = 0$).
1
Compute Partial Derivatives at $(1, 2)$
$$f(x, y) = (x - y)^2 \implies f_x = 2(x - y), \qquad f_y = -2(x - y)$$ $$f_x(1, 2) = 2(1 - 2) = -2, \qquad f_y(1, 2) = -2(1 - 2) = 2$$
2
Assemble Explicit Tangent Plane and Match $+1$ Constant
$$\begin{aligned} z &= f(1, 2) + f_x(1, 2)(x - 1) + f_y(1, 2)(y - 2) \\ z &= 1 - 2(x - 1) + 2(y - 2) \\ z &= 1 - 2x + 2 + 2y - 4 = -2x + 2y - 1 \\ 2x - 2y + z + 1 &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

Comparing with $ax + by + cz + 1 = 0$ gives $a = 2,\; b = -2,\; c = 1$, so $a + b + c = 2 - 2 + 1 = 1$.

Verification: Point $(1, 2, 1)$ satisfies $2(1) - 2(2) + 1 + 1 = 0$, and normal $\langle 2, -2, 1 \rangle$ equals $-\langle -2, 2, -1 \rangle = -\nabla(f - z)|_{(1,2,1)}$.
Example 3 • Surface Intersection Curve • Textbook p. 937 Ex. 31 & Alhumaidy Notes

Tangent Line Where a Sphere Meets a Plane

Find parametric equations for the tangent line to the curve of intersection of the sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 14$ and the plane $x - y - z = 0$ at $P_0(3, 1, 2)$.
Final Answer: $x = 3 + t, \quad y = 1 + 5t, \quad z = 2 - 4t \quad (-\infty < t < \infty)$.
1
Find Both Gradients at $P_0(3, 1, 2)$
$$F(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 14 \implies \nabla F(3, 1, 2) = \langle 6, 2, 4 \rangle$$ $$G(x, y, z) = x - y - z \implies \nabla G(3, 1, 2) = \langle 1, -1, -1 \rangle$$
2
Compute Cross Product $\nabla F(P_0) \times \nabla G(P_0)$
$$\begin{aligned} \nabla F \times \nabla G &= \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 6 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & -1 & -1 \end{vmatrix} \\ &= \big(2(-1) - 4(-1)\big)\mathbf{i} - \big(6(-1) - 4(1)\big)\mathbf{j} + \big(6(-1) - 2(1)\big)\mathbf{k} \\ &= \langle 2,\; 10,\; -8 \rangle = 2\langle 1,\; 5,\; -4 \rangle \end{aligned}$$

Using simplified direction $\langle 1, 5, -4 \rangle$ through $(3, 1, 2)$ gives $x = 3 + t,\; y = 1 + 5t,\; z = 2 - 4t$.

Verification: Both dot products vanish: $\langle 1, 5, -4 \rangle \cdot \langle 6, 2, 4 \rangle = 6 + 10 - 16 = 0$, and $\langle 1, 5, -4 \rangle \cdot \langle 1, -1, -1 \rangle = 1 - 5 + 4 = 0$.

Core Concept

1. Mountain Peaks, Valleys & Mountain Passes (Critical Points)

Intuitive Picture: Picture the graph of $z = f(x, y)$ as a 3D mountain range. If you stand at the top of a smooth peak (a relative maximum) or the bottom of a smooth crater (a relative minimum), the ground beneath your feet must be completely level in every compass direction — if it sloped even slightly north or east, you could take a tiny step higher or lower! A level tangent plane means both partial derivatives vanish simultaneously.

Definition: Critical Point of $f(x, y)$

An interior point $(x_0, y_0)$ of the domain of $f$ is a critical point if either:

  1. $f_x(x_0, y_0) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad f_y(x_0, y_0) = 0$  (horizontal tangent plane), OR
  2. At least one of $f_x(x_0, y_0)$ or $f_y(x_0, y_0)$ does not exist (e.g., the sharp tip of a cone $z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ at $(0, 0)$).

Fermat's Theorem in 2D: Every relative extremum occurs at a critical point — but not every critical point is an extremum!

The Brand-New 3D Behavior: Saddle Points

In 2D landscapes, a point with a flat horizontal tangent plane can be neither a peak nor a valley: it can be a mountain pass (saddle point), just like the middle of a horse saddle ($z = y^2 - x^2$ at $(0, 0)$). Walking North-South ($x = 0$), you climb uphill ($z = y^2 > 0$); walking East-West ($y = 0$), you plunge downhill ($z = -x^2 < 0$). Because every tiny neighborhood contains points both higher and lower than $(x_0, y_0)$, it is a saddle point — never a max or min.

Core Concept

2. The Second Partials Test: Why $D = f_{xx}f_{yy} - [f_{xy}]^2$ Works

Intuitive Picture: In Calc I, $f''(x_0) > 0$ guaranteed a local minimum because a 1D curve only bends in one direction. In 2D, checking $f_{xx} > 0$ (concave up along $x$) and $f_{yy} > 0$ (concave up along $y$) is NOT enough! Why? Because the surface can twist diagonally, measured by the mixed partial $f_{xy}$. If the diagonal twist $[f_{xy}]^2$ is stronger than the combined bending $f_{xx}f_{yy}$, the surface plunges downward along a $45^\circ$ diagonal — creating a hidden saddle point! For example, $z = x^2 + 4xy + y^2$ has $f_{xx} = 2 > 0$ and $f_{yy} = 2 > 0$, yet along the diagonal $y = -x$ we get $z = -2x^2 < 0$!

The Second Partials Test (Hessian Discriminant $D$)

Let $(x_0, y_0)$ be a critical point where $f_x(x_0, y_0) = 0$ and $f_y(x_0, y_0) = 0$, and assume the second partial derivatives are continuous near $(x_0, y_0)$. Define the discriminant:

$$D(x_0, y_0) = \begin{vmatrix} f_{xx}(x_0, y_0) & f_{xy}(x_0, y_0) \\ f_{yx}(x_0, y_0) & f_{yy}(x_0, y_0) \end{vmatrix} = f_{xx}(x_0, y_0)\,f_{yy}(x_0, y_0) - \big[f_{xy}(x_0, y_0)\big]^2$$
Sign of $D$ Sign of $f_{xx}$ (or $f_{yy}$) Classification 3D Shape
$D > 0$ $f_{xx}(x_0, y_0) > 0$ Relative Minimum Upward bowl (concave up in every direction)
$D > 0$ $f_{xx}(x_0, y_0) < 0$ Relative Maximum Downward dome (concave down in every direction)
$D < 0$ Irrelevant! Saddle Point Curves up along one axis, down along another
$D = 0$ Irrelevant! Inconclusive Must inspect $f(x,y)$ directly (could be min, max, or saddle)

Memory shortcut: Whenever $D > 0$, we must have $f_{xx}f_{yy} > [f_{xy}]^2 \ge 0$, which proves $f_{xx}$ and $f_{yy}$ have the exact same sign! So checking $f_{xx}$ alone tells you whether the entire bowl opens up ($+$) or down ($-$).

Core Concept

3. Absolute Extrema on Closed, Bounded Regions

Intuitive Picture: Imagine a fenced national park $R$ on the $xy$-plane. "Closed" means the fence itself is included; "bounded" means the park has finite size. The Extreme Value Theorem guarantees that any continuous function $f(x, y)$ on $R$ must achieve both a highest elevation (absolute maximum) and a lowest elevation (absolute minimum). Those champions can hide in only two places: strictly inside the park (interior critical points) or along the fence (boundary edges and corners).

The 3-Stage Absolute Extrema Algorithm (No $D$-Test Needed!)

  1. Stage 1 — Interior Candidates: Solve $f_x = 0$ and $f_y = 0$ (plus any DNE points). Keep only the critical points that lie strictly inside $R$ — immediately discard any point outside $R$!
  2. Stage 2 — Boundary Candidates: Parameterize or substitute each piece of the boundary $\partial R$ into $f(x, y)$ to turn it into a 1-variable function $g(t)$. Take $g'(t) = 0$ to find 1D critical points along each edge, AND always list every corner / endpoint where boundary segments meet.
  3. Stage 3 — The Final Showdown: Plug every single candidate from Stage 1 and Stage 2 into the original function $f(x, y)$. The largest number is the absolute maximum; the smallest number is the absolute minimum.

KFUPM Exam Traps to Avoid in Section 13.8

  • Trap 1 — Cancelling Variables Instead of Factoring: When solving $6xy = 0$ or $2xy - 2x = 0$, NEVER divide both sides by $x$! Factor as $2x(y - 1) = 0 \implies x = 0 \text{ or } y = 1$. Dividing by $x$ silently erases entire critical points.
  • Trap 2 — Forgetting to Check Whether a Critical Point Lies Inside $R$: On absolute extrema problems, a critical point outside $R$ can give a fake "minimum" that isn't even in the allowed domain! Always test each critical point against all boundary inequalities.
  • Trap 3 — Checking Only Corners on a Triangle or Rectangle: The absolute extremum frequently occurs at a 1D critical point in the middle of a slanted edge (see Worked Example 3 below!). You must reduce $f$ to $g(t)$ on every edge and differentiate.
Worked Example

Worked Example 1: Finding & Classifying Critical Points of $f(x, y) = x^3 + y^3 - 3xy$

Find all critical points of $f(x, y) = x^3 + y^3 - 3xy$ and classify each as a relative maximum, relative minimum, or saddle point.
Final Answer: $(0, 0)$ is a saddle point ($f(0, 0) = 0$); $(1, 1)$ is a relative minimum with $f(1, 1) = -1$.
1
Set Both First Partial Derivatives Equal to Zero

Since $f$ is a polynomial, its partials exist everywhere, so critical points satisfy $f_x = 0$ and $f_y = 0$:

$$\begin{aligned} f_x &= 3x^2 - 3y = 0 \implies y = x^2 \\ f_y &= 3y^2 - 3x = 0 \implies x = y^2 \end{aligned}$$
2
Substitute and Factor Completely (Never Divide by $x$!)

Substitute $y = x^2$ from the first equation into $x = y^2$:

$$x = (x^2)^2 = x^4 \implies x^4 - x = 0 \implies x(x^3 - 1) = 0$$

Factor the difference of cubes $x^3 - 1 = (x - 1)(x^2 + x + 1)$:

$$x(x - 1)(x^2 + x + 1) = 0$$

Since $x^2 + x + 1 = 0$ has discriminant $1^2 - 4(1)(1) = -3 < 0$, it has no real roots. Thus the real solutions are:

  • $x = 0 \implies y = 0^2 = 0 \implies \text{critical point } (0, 0)$.
  • $x = 1 \implies y = 1^2 = 1 \implies \text{critical point } (1, 1)$.
3
Compute Second Partials and the Discriminant $D(x, y)$
$$f_{xx} = 6x, \qquad f_{yy} = 6y, \qquad f_{xy} = -3$$ $$D(x, y) = f_{xx}f_{yy} - (f_{xy})^2 = (6x)(6y) - (-3)^2 = 36xy - 9$$
4
Test Each Critical Point
  • At $(0, 0)$: $D(0, 0) = 36(0)(0) - 9 = -9 < 0 \implies$ Saddle point at $(0, 0, 0)$.
  • At $(1, 1)$: $D(1, 1) = 36(1)(1) - 9 = 27 > 0$ and $f_{xx}(1, 1) = 6(1) = 6 > 0 \implies$ Relative minimum at $(1, 1)$ with value $f(1, 1) = 1^3 + 1^3 - 3(1)(1) = -1$.

Physical / Geometric Meaning

Notice that $(1, 1)$ sits at the bottom of a local basin of depth $-1$, whereas the origin $(0, 0)$ is a pass where the diagonal twist $f_{xy} = -3$ forces the surface to dip below $z = 0$ along $y = x$ (for $x > 0$, $z = 2x^3 - 3x^2 < 0$) while rising above $z = 0$ along $y = -x$ ($z = 3x^2 > 0$).

Find and classify all critical points of $f(x, y) = x^3 - 3x + 3xy^2$.
Final Answer: Relative minimum at $(1, 0)$ ($f = -2$); relative maximum at $(-1, 0)$ ($f = 2$); saddle points at $(0, 1)$ and $(0, -1)$ ($f = 0$).
1
Compute $f_x = 0$ and $f_y = 0$

2
Branch Cleanly on Equation (2)
  • Case A ($x = 0$): Substitute into (1): $0^2 + y^2 = 1 \implies y = \pm 1$. This gives $(0, 1)$ and $(0, -1)$.
  • Case B ($y = 0$): Substitute into (1): $x^2 + 0^2 = 1 \implies x = \pm 1$. This gives $(1, 0)$ and $(-1, 0)$.
3
Build the Discriminant $D(x, y)$ and Classify All 4 Points
$$f_{xx} = 6x, \qquad f_{yy} = 6x, \qquad f_{xy} = 6y$$ $$D(x, y) = (6x)(6x) - (6y)^2 = 36(x^2 - y^2)$$

  • At $(-1, 0)$: $D = 36(1 - 0) = 36 > 0$, $f_{xx} = -6 < 0 \implies$ Relative maximum, $f(-1, 0) = -1 + 3 + 0 = 2$.
  • At $(0, 1)$: $D = 36(0 - 1) = -36 < 0 \implies$ Saddle point, $f(0, 1) = 0$.
  • At $(0, -1)$: $D = 36(0 - 1) = -36 < 0 \implies$ Saddle point, $f(0, -1) = 0$.

Physical / Geometric Meaning

All four critical points sit neatly at the four compass points of the unit circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$: a dome peak in the West $(-1, 0)$, a valley floor in the East $(1, 0)$, and two symmetric mountain passes at the North and South poles $(0, \pm 1)$!

Worked Example 3: Absolute Extrema on a Closed Triangular Region

Find the absolute maximum and absolute minimum values of $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - 4x - 2y + 5$ on the closed triangular region $R$ with vertices $(0, 0)$, $(2, 0)$, and $(0, 2)$.
Final Answer: Absolute maximum is $5$ (attained at both $(0, 0)$ and $(0, 2)$); absolute minimum is $\dfrac{1}{2}$ (attained on the hypotenuse at $\left(\dfrac{3}{2},\, \dfrac{1}{2}\right)$).
1
Describe $R$ and Check Interior Critical Points

The triangle $R$ is defined by $x \ge 0$, $y \ge 0$, and $x + y \le 2$. Compute interior critical points:

$$f_x = 2x - 4 = 0 \implies x = 2, \qquad f_y = 2y - 2 = 0 \implies y = 1$$

Test $(2, 1)$ against the hypotenuse condition $x + y \le 2$: here $2 + 1 = 3 > 2$, so $(2, 1)$ lies OUTSIDE $R$! We immediately discard $(2, 1)$. Thus there are no interior candidates.

2
Test Boundary Edge $L_1$: Bottom Edge ($y = 0,\; 0 \le x \le 2$)

Substitute $y = 0$ into $f(x, y)$:

$$g_1(x) = f(x, 0) = x^2 - 4x + 5, \qquad g_1'(x) = 2x - 4 = 0 \implies x = 2$$

This gives the endpoints $(0, 0)$ and $(2, 0)$:

$$f(0, 0) = 5, \qquad f(2, 0) = 4 - 8 + 5 = 1$$
3
Test Boundary Edge $L_2$: Left Edge ($x = 0,\; 0 \le y \le 2$)

Substitute $x = 0$ into $f(x, y)$:

$$g_2(y) = f(0, y) = y^2 - 2y + 5, \qquad g_2'(y) = 2y - 2 = 0 \implies y = 1 \in (0, 2)$$

Evaluate the edge critical point $(0, 1)$ and the top vertex $(0, 2)$:

$$f(0, 1) = 1 - 2 + 5 = 4, \qquad f(0, 2) = 4 - 4 + 5 = 5$$
4
Test Boundary Edge $L_3$: Slanted Hypotenuse ($y = 2 - x,\; 0 \le x \le 2$)

Substitute $y = 2 - x$ into $f(x, y)$ and expand carefully:

Differentiate and solve $g_3'(x) = 0$:

$$g_3'(x) = 4x - 6 = 0 \implies x = \frac{3}{2} \in (0, 2)$$

Then $y = 2 - \frac{3}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$, giving the hypotenuse candidate $\left(\frac{3}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right)$:

$$f\!\left(\frac{3}{2},\, \frac{1}{2}\right) = 2\!\left(\frac{9}{4}\right) - 6\!\left(\frac{3}{2}\right) + 5 = \frac{9}{2} - 9 + 5 = \frac{1}{2}$$
5
Compare Every Candidate Value

List all candidates: $f(0, 0) = 5$, $f(0, 2) = 5$, $f(0, 1) = 4$, $f(2, 0) = 1$, and $f\!\left(\frac{3}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{2}$. Therefore, the absolute maximum is $5$ and the absolute minimum is $\frac{1}{2}$.

Core Concept

1. The Optimization Blueprint: Turning Word Problems into Calculus

Intuitive Picture: Every optimization problem is a tug-of-war between a goal (make volume, profit, or revenue as large as possible; or make cardboard area, cost, or distance as small as possible) and a physical rule (a fixed volume, a fixed plane, or staying on a surface). The physical rule ties your three variables $x, y, z$ together so that once you pick $x$ and $y$, the third variable $z$ is locked in automatically. Eliminating $z$ turns the word problem into a standard two-variable critical-point problem from Section 13.8!

The 5-Step Multivariable Optimization Protocol

  1. Assign Variables & Physical Domain: Label dimensions or coordinates ($x, y, z$) and note physical bounds (e.g., lengths satisfy $x > 0,\; y > 0,\; z > 0$).
  2. Separate Objective vs. Constraint: Write down the Primary (Objective) Equation (the quantity to maximize or minimize) and the Secondary (Constraint) Equation (the fixed number or surface equation).
  3. Eliminate One Variable: Solve the constraint for the easiest variable (typically $z$) and substitute it into the objective to get a pure 2-variable function $f(x, y)$.
  4. Solve $f_x(x, y) = 0$ and $f_y(x, y) = 0$: Keep only solutions that satisfy the physical domain (discard zero or negative dimensions when lengths must be strictly positive).
  5. Verify & Answer the Exact Question: Confirm maximum vs. minimum using the Second Partials Test ($D > 0$ with $f_{xx} < 0$ for max, $f_{xx} > 0$ for min), and double-check whether the problem asked for the dimensions, the point $(x, y, z)$, or the optimal value!
Core Concept

2. Two Signature KFUPM Tricks: The $D^2$ Trick & Box Symmetry

Intuitive Picture: Two ideas appear over and over on KFUPM exams because they save massive amounts of algebra while revealing deep geometric intuition: minimizing squared distance instead of distance, and recognizing how lidless vs. closed boxes balance their face areas.

Trick A: The Squared-Distance ($D^2$) Trick

The Euclidean distance from a variable point $(x, y, z)$ to a fixed point $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ is:

$$d = \sqrt{(x - x_0)^2 + (y - y_0)^2 + (z - z_0)^2}$$

Differentiating a square root creates ugly fractional denominators. Because $u \mapsto u^2$ is strictly increasing for $u \ge 0$, the exact same point $(x, y, z)$ minimizes $d$ and minimizes its square:

$$f(x, y, z) = d^2 = (x - x_0)^2 + (y - y_0)^2 + (z - z_0)^2$$

Eliminate $z$ (or $z^2$ directly!) using the surface equation, minimize $f(x, y)$ with zero radicals, and take the square root at the very end if the question asks for the shortest distance $d_{\min} = \sqrt{f_{\min}}$.

Trick B: Open-Top vs. Closed Box Geometry

  • Closed Box (6 faces): Surface area is $S = 2xy + 2xz + 2yz$. Every axis is treated identically, so for a fixed volume $V$, the minimum-area box is a perfect cube ($x = y = z$).
  • Open-Top Box (5 faces — no lid!): Surface area is $S = xy + 2xz + 2yz$. Because the base has only one face instead of two, expanding the base is half as expensive as raising the walls — so the optimal box always has a square base ($x = y$) with height half the base ($z = \frac{1}{2}x$), making the base area equal to each opposite pair of walls ($xy = 2xz = 2yz$)!

KFUPM Exam Traps to Avoid in Section 13.9

  • Trap 1 — Forgetting the Square Root After Using $D^2$: Students correctly minimize $f = d^2 = 10$ and accidentally bubble in $10$ instead of $d_{\min} = \sqrt{10}$! Always highlight whether the question asks for the closest point or the shortest distance.
  • Trap 2 — Using $2xy$ for an Open-Top Box: Read carefully for words like "open at the top", "without a lid", or "different material cost per square meter on the base" — a single missing factor of $2$ shifts the entire answer.
  • Trap 3 — Dropping the Second Derivative / Domain Check: If $V(x, y) = \frac{1}{3}xy(6 - x - 2y)$ gives critical points with $x = 0$ or $y = 0$, those correspond to flat zero-volume boxes ($V = 0$), NOT the maximum! Always restrict to $x > 0,\; y > 0,\; z > 0$.
Worked Example

Worked Example 1: Open-Top Rectangular Box of Minimum Surface Area

An open-top rectangular box (no lid) is to be constructed with a fixed volume of $32\text{ m}^3$. Find the dimensions $(x, y, z)$ that minimize the total surface area of material used, and compute that minimum surface area.
Final Answer: Dimensions are $4\text{ m} \times 4\text{ m} \times 2\text{ m}$ (base $x = 4$, $y = 4$, height $z = 2$), giving a minimum surface area of $48\text{ m}^2$.
1
Set Up Objective (Surface Area) and Constraint (Volume)

Let $x, y > 0$ be the base length and width, and $z > 0$ be the height. Because the box has no lid, it has 1 bottom face ($xy$) and 4 vertical walls ($2xz + 2yz$):

$$\text{Minimize } S = xy + 2xz + 2yz \qquad \text{subject to } V = xyz = 32$$
2
Eliminate $z$ to Obtain $S(x, y)$

From the volume constraint, $z = \dfrac{32}{xy}$. Substitute into $S$:

$$S(x, y) = xy + 2x\!\left(\frac{32}{xy}\right) + 2y\!\left(\frac{32}{xy}\right) = xy + \frac{64}{y} + \frac{64}{x}$$
3
Find the Critical Point ($S_x = 0,\; S_y = 0$)

Compute both partial derivatives and set them to zero:

$$\begin{aligned} S_x &= y - \frac{64}{x^2} = 0 \implies x^2 y = 64 \quad &(1) \\ S_y &= x - \frac{64}{y^2} = 0 \implies xy^2 = 64 \quad &(2) \end{aligned}$$

Since $x > 0$ and $y > 0$, divide equation (1) by equation (2):

$$\frac{x^2 y}{xy^2} = \frac{64}{64} \implies \frac{x}{y} = 1 \implies x = y$$

Substitute $y = x$ back into equation (1):

$$x^2(x) = 64 \implies x^3 = 64 \implies x = 4$$

Therefore $y = 4$, and $z = \dfrac{32}{(4)(4)} = \dfrac{32}{16} = 2$.

4
Verify Minimum via Second Partials Test & Compute $S_{\min}$
$$S_{xx} = \frac{128}{x^3}, \qquad S_{yy} = \frac{128}{y^3}, \qquad S_{xy} = 1$$

At $(4, 4)$: $S_{xx} = \dfrac{128}{64} = 2 > 0$, $S_{yy} = 2$, and $S_{xy} = 1$, so:

$$D(4, 4) = (2)(2) - 1^2 = 3 > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad S_{xx}(4, 4) = 2 > 0 \implies \text{Minimum!}$$

Finally, compute the minimum surface area:

$$S_{\min} = (4)(4) + \frac{64}{4} + \frac{64}{4} = 16 + 16 + 16 = 48\text{ m}^2$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning

Notice the $16 + 16 + 16 = 48\text{ m}^2$ split: the optimal open-top box allocates exactly one-third of its cardboard to the bottom ($16\text{ m}^2$), one-third to the front-and-back walls ($16\text{ m}^2$), and one-third to the left-and-right walls ($16\text{ m}^2$), giving a height exactly half the base width ($z = 2 = \frac{1}{2}x$).

Worked Example

Worked Example 2: Maximum Volume Box in the First Octant Under a Plane

Find the maximum volume of a rectangular box in the first octant with three faces in the coordinate planes and one vertex lying on the plane $x + 2y + 3z = 6$.
Final Answer: Maximum volume is $V_{\max} = \dfrac{4}{3}$ cubic units, attained at dimensions $x = 2,\; y = 1,\; z = \dfrac{2}{3}$.
1
Express Volume $V$ as a Function of Two Variables $(x, y)$

Because the box rests against the three coordinate planes with opposite corner $(x, y, z)$ in the first octant ($x > 0,\; y > 0,\; z > 0$), its volume is $V = xyz$. Solve the plane constraint for $z$:

$$x + 2y + 3z = 6 \implies z = \frac{6 - x - 2y}{3}$$

Substitute into $V = xyz$ and expand for clean differentiation:

$$V(x, y) = xy\!\left(\frac{6 - x - 2y}{3}\right) = 2xy - \frac{1}{3}x^2 y - \frac{2}{3}xy^2$$
2
Set $V_x = 0$ and $V_y = 0$ and Solve for Positive $(x, y)$
$$\begin{aligned} V_x &= 2y - \frac{2}{3}xy - \frac{2}{3}y^2 = \frac{2}{3}y(3 - x - y) = 0 \\ V_y &= 2x - \frac{1}{3}x^2 - \frac{4}{3}xy = \frac{1}{3}x(6 - x - 4y) = 0 \end{aligned}$$

Since $x > 0$ and $y > 0$ for a non-degenerate box, the factors inside the parentheses must vanish:

$$\begin{cases} x + y = 3 \quad &(1) \\ x + 4y = 6 \quad &(2) \end{cases}$$

Subtract equation (1) from equation (2): $(x + 4y) - (x + y) = 6 - 3 \implies 3y = 3 \implies y = 1$.

Back-substitute: $x = 3 - 1 = 2$, and $z = \dfrac{6 - 2 - 2(1)}{3} = \dfrac{2}{3}$.

3
Verify Maximum via Second Partials Test & Evaluate $V_{\max}$
$$V_{xx} = -\frac{2}{3}y, \qquad V_{yy} = -\frac{4}{3}x, \qquad V_{xy} = 2 - \frac{2}{3}x - \frac{4}{3}y$$

At $(2, 1)$: $V_{xx} = -\dfrac{2}{3} < 0$, $V_{yy} = -\dfrac{8}{3}$, and $V_{xy} = 2 - \dfrac{4}{3} - \dfrac{4}{3} = -\dfrac{2}{3}$.

$$D(2, 1) = \left(-\frac{2}{3}\right)\!\left(-\frac{8}{3}\right) - \left(-\frac{2}{3}\right)^2 = \frac{16}{9} - \frac{4}{9} = \frac{12}{9} = \frac{4}{3} > 0$$

Since $D > 0$ and $V_{xx} < 0$, $(2, 1)$ yields a strict maximum! Thus:

$$V_{\max} = (2)(1)\!\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) = \frac{4}{3}$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning (KFUPM Speed-Check Formula!)

For ANY box in the first octant under a plane $ax + by + cz = d$ ($a,b,c,d > 0$), the optimal corner always splits the constant $d$ equally into thirds: $ax = by = cz = \frac{d}{3}$! Check our answer: $1(2) = 2$, $2(1) = 2$, $3\!\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) = 2$, and $2 + 2 + 2 = 6$. The maximum volume is always $\frac{d^3}{27abc} = \frac{6^3}{27(1)(2)(3)} = \frac{216}{162} = \frac{4}{3}$!

Worked Example 3: Shortest Distance from a Point to a Cone ($D^2$ Trick)

Find the point(s) on the double cone $z^2 = x^2 + y^2$ that are closest to the point $P(4, 2, 0)$, and find the shortest distance from $P$ to the cone.
Final Answer: The two closest points are $\big(2,\, 1,\, \sqrt{5}\big)$ and $\big(2,\, 1,\, -\sqrt{5}\big)$, and the shortest distance is $d_{\min} = \sqrt{10}$.
1
Apply the $D^2$ Trick and Eliminate $z^2$ Directly!

The squared distance from an arbitrary point $(x, y, z)$ on the cone to $P(4, 2, 0)$ is:

$$d^2 = (x - 4)^2 + (y - 2)^2 + (z - 0)^2 = (x - 4)^2 + (y - 2)^2 + z^2$$

Because the constraint directly gives $z^2 = x^2 + y^2$, substitute $z^2$ right in — no square roots anywhere!

$$\begin{aligned} f(x, y) &= (x - 4)^2 + (y - 2)^2 + x^2 + y^2 \\ &= (x^2 - 8x + 16) + (y^2 - 4y + 4) + x^2 + y^2 \\ &= 2x^2 - 8x + 2y^2 - 4y + 20 \end{aligned}$$
2
Find the Critical Point ($f_x = 0,\; f_y = 0$)
$$f_x = 4x - 8 = 0 \implies x = 2, \qquad f_y = 4y - 4 = 0 \implies y = 1$$

Now solve the cone equation for $z$:

$$z^2 = x^2 + y^2 = 2^2 + 1^2 = 5 \implies z = \pm\sqrt{5}$$

Thus there are two symmetric candidate points: $(2, 1, \sqrt{5})$ on the upper cone and $(2, 1, -\sqrt{5})$ on the lower cone.

3
Confirm Minimum and Take the Square Root for $d_{\min}$

Second partials: $f_{xx} = 4 > 0$, $f_{yy} = 4$, $f_{xy} = 0$, so $D = (4)(4) - 0^2 = 16 > 0$ everywhere. Hence $(2, 1)$ gives the global minimum of $f = d^2$:

$$f(2, 1) = (2 - 4)^2 + (1 - 2)^2 + (\pm\sqrt{5})^2 = 4 + 1 + 5 = 10$$

Crucial final step: Remember $f = d^2 = 10$, so take the square root to get the actual distance:

$$d_{\min} = \sqrt{10}$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning

Because $P(4, 2, 0)$ lies right in the horizontal $xy$-plane while the cone is symmetric above and below that plane, the upper and lower nappes are equally close to $P$! Even better: the displacement vector from $P(4, 2, 0)$ to $(2, 1, \sqrt{5})$ is $\langle -2, -1, \sqrt{5} \rangle$, which is parallel to the cone's normal vector $\nabla(x^2 + y^2 - z^2) = \langle 4, 2, -2\sqrt{5} \rangle$ — the shortest path always hits a smooth surface at $90^\circ$!

Worked Example 4: Two-Product Revenue & Profit Maximization

A company produces two competing wireless earbud models. The demand equations linking unit prices $p_1, p_2$ (in dollars) to production levels $x_1, x_2$ (in thousands of units) are $p_1 = 50 - 2x_1 - x_2$ and $p_2 = 40 - x_1 - 3x_2$. If the total production cost is $C(x_1, x_2) = 10x_1 + 10x_2 + 25$ (in thousands of dollars), find the quantities $(x_1, x_2)$ that maximize total profit $P$, and find the maximum profit.
Final Answer: Produce $x_1 = 9$ thousand units of Model 1 and $x_2 = 2$ thousand units of Model 2 for a maximum profit of $185$ thousand dollars (USD 185,000).
1
Build the Revenue Function $R$ and Profit Function $P = R - C$

Total revenue is price times quantity summed over both products: $R(x_1, x_2) = x_1 p_1 + x_2 p_2$:

$$\begin{aligned} R(x_1, x_2) &= x_1(50 - 2x_1 - x_2) + x_2(40 - x_1 - 3x_2) \\ &= 50x_1 - 2x_1^2 - x_1 x_2 + 40x_2 - x_1 x_2 - 3x_2^2 \\ &= 50x_1 + 40x_2 - 2x_1^2 - 2x_1 x_2 - 3x_2^2 \end{aligned}$$

Subtract the total cost $C(x_1, x_2) = 10x_1 + 10x_2 + 25$ to get profit $P = R - C$:

$$P(x_1, x_2) = 40x_1 + 30x_2 - 2x_1^2 - 2x_1 x_2 - 3x_2^2 - 25$$
2
Set Both Marginal Profits to Zero ($P_{x_1} = 0,\; P_{x_2} = 0$)
$$\begin{aligned} P_{x_1} &= 40 - 4x_1 - 2x_2 = 0 \implies 2x_1 + x_2 = 20 \quad &(1) \\ P_{x_2} &= 30 - 2x_1 - 6x_2 = 0 \implies x_1 + 3x_2 = 15 \quad &(2) \end{aligned}$$

From equation (1), $x_2 = 20 - 2x_1$. Substitute into equation (2):

$$x_1 + 3(20 - 2x_1) = 15 \implies x_1 + 60 - 6x_1 = 15 \implies -5x_1 = -45 \implies x_1 = 9$$

Then $x_2 = 20 - 2(9) = 2$.

3
Verify Maximum via Second Partials Test & Compute $P_{\max}$
$$P_{x_1 x_1} = -4, \qquad P_{x_2 x_2} = -6, \qquad P_{x_1 x_2} = -2$$ $$D = (-4)(-6) - (-2)^2 = 24 - 4 = 20 > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad P_{x_1 x_1} = -4 < 0 \implies \text{Global Maximum!}$$

Evaluate $P(9, 2)$:

$$\begin{aligned} P(9, 2) &= 40(9) + 30(2) - 2(9)^2 - 2(9)(2) - 3(2)^2 - 25 \\ &= 360 + 60 - 162 - 36 - 12 - 25 = 420 - 235 = 185 \end{aligned}$$

Thus the maximum profit is $185$ thousand dollars (USD 185,000).

Physical / Geometric Meaning

Because the two earbud models cannibalize each other's demand (the negative cross-terms $-x_2$ and $-x_1$), producing too much of Model 2 hurts Model 1's price. At the optimal mix $(9, 2)$, the company prices Model 1 at $p_1 = 30$ and Model 2 at $p_2 = 25$, sitting squarely at the apex of a downward-opening elliptic paraboloid in profit space.

Core Concept

1. The Geometric "Aha!" Moment: Why $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$

Intuitive Picture: In Section 13.9 we eliminated a variable from a constraint — but what if the constraint is $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 36$ or $x^3 + y^3 = 10$, where solving for $z$ introduces awful square roots or is algebraically impossible? Joseph-Louis Lagrange discovered an astonishingly elegant geometric shortcut that keeps complete symmetry across all variables without ever solving for one variable!

The Topographic Map & Hiking Trail Analogy

Picture a topographic contour map showing elevation curves $f(x, y) = c$ (say $c = 10, 20, 30, \dots$) drawn as rings around a mountain. Now paint a closed hiking loop $g(x, y) = k$ onto the map. You are strictly forced to stay on that loop, and you want the highest and lowest elevation you ever reach.

  • Whenever your trail CROSSES a contour line at an angle: One direction along the trail goes to higher elevation ($c$ increases) and the opposite direction goes lower ($c$ decreases). Therefore, a crossing point can never be the highest or lowest point on the trail!
  • At the exact highest or lowest point on the trail: The trail can no longer cross into higher (or lower) contour rings — it must just kiss (be tangent to) the contour curve $f(x, y) = c$!
  • Translate tangency into gradients: Two curves are tangent at a point if and only if their normal vectors are parallel. Since $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to the level curve $f(x, y) = c$ and $\nabla g$ is perpendicular to the constraint curve $g(x, y) = k$, parallelism means one gradient is a scalar multiple of the other: $$\nabla f(x, y) = \lambda\,\nabla g(x, y)$$

Method of Lagrange Multipliers (Single Constraint)

To find the maximum and minimum values of $f$ subject to $g = k$ (assuming $\nabla g \neq \mathbf{0}$ at the extremum):

  • In 2D (3 equations in 3 unknowns $x, y, \lambda$): $$f_x(x, y) = \lambda\, g_x(x, y), \qquad f_y(x, y) = \lambda\, g_y(x, y), \qquad g(x, y) = k$$
  • In 3D (4 equations in 4 unknowns $x, y, z, \lambda$): $$f_x = \lambda\, g_x, \qquad f_y = \lambda\, g_y, \qquad f_z = \lambda\, g_z, \qquad g(x, y, z) = k$$
  • Final Selection: Evaluate the original objective $f$ at every candidate point found. On a closed, bounded constraint (like a circle, ellipse, or sphere), the largest value is the absolute maximum and the smallest is the absolute minimum — no Second Partials Test required!
Core Concept

2. Two Constraints in 3D ($\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g + \mu \nabla h$) & Zero-Division Traps

Intuitive Picture: What if you must optimize $f(x, y, z)$ along a 1D curve $C$ in 3D space formed by intersecting two surfaces $g(x, y, z) = k_1$ and $h(x, y, z) = k_2$ (such as a tilted plane slicing a cylinder into an ellipse)? At any extremum on $C$, the tangent vector $\mathbf{v}$ to $C$ must be perpendicular to $\nabla f$ — otherwise walking along $C$ would increase or decrease $f$. Because $\nabla g$ and $\nabla h$ span the entire 2D plane perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$, the vector $\nabla f$ must lie inside that same plane, meaning it is a linear combination of $\nabla g$ and $\nabla h$!

Method of Lagrange Multipliers with Two Constraints

To extremize $f(x, y, z)$ subject to BOTH $g(x, y, z) = k_1$ and $h(x, y, z) = k_2$ (assuming $\nabla g$ and $\nabla h$ are not parallel):

$$\nabla f(x, y, z) = \lambda\,\nabla g(x, y, z) + \mu\,\nabla h(x, y, z)$$

This gives a system of 5 equations in 5 unknowns $(x, y, z, \lambda, \mu)$:

$$\begin{cases} f_x = \lambda g_x + \mu h_x \\ f_y = \lambda g_y + \mu h_y \\ f_z = \lambda g_z + \mu h_z \\ g(x, y, z) = k_1 \\ h(x, y, z) = k_2 \end{cases}$$

KFUPM Exam Traps to Avoid in Section 13.10

  • Trap 1 — The Deadly Division-by-Zero Trap: Suppose your system contains $4y = 2\lambda y$. If you divide both sides by $y$ to claim $\lambda = 2$, you have silently assumed $y \neq 0$ and deleted the entire $y = 0$ solution branch! ALWAYS move to one side and factor: $2y(2 - \lambda) = 0 \implies$ Case 1: $y = 0$ OR Case 2: $\lambda = 2$. On KFUPM exams, the $y = 0$ branch is very frequently the true global maximum or minimum!
  • Trap 2 — Forgetting the Constraint Equation $g = k$: The gradient equation $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ only determines the direction / ratio between variables (e.g., $y = 2x$). You must plug those ratios back into $g = k$ to find actual numbers.
  • Trap 3 — Confusing $\lambda$ with the Optimal Value: The multiplier $\lambda$ is merely an auxiliary bridge (and may never even need to be found explicitly if you eliminate it by dividing non-zero equations). Always evaluate $f$ at the coordinates $(x, y, z)$!
Worked Example

Worked Example 1: 2D Circle Constraint & The Zero-Division Branching Trap

Use Lagrange multipliers to find the absolute maximum and absolute minimum values of $f(x, y) = x^2 + 2y^2 - 2x$ subject to the constraint $x^2 + y^2 = 4$.
Final Answer: Absolute maximum is $9$ (at $(-1, \sqrt{3})$ and $(-1, -\sqrt{3})$); absolute minimum is $0$ (at $(2, 0)$).
1
Set Up $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ and the Constraint

Let $g(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 = 4$. Compute both gradients:

$$\nabla f = \langle 2x - 2,\; 4y \rangle, \qquad \nabla g = \langle 2x,\; 2y \rangle$$

Writing out $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ along with $g = 4$ gives:

$$\begin{cases} 2x - 2 = 2\lambda x \implies x - 1 = \lambda x \quad &(1) \\ 4y = 2\lambda y \implies 2y(2 - \lambda) = 0 \quad &(2) \\ x^2 + y^2 = 4 \quad &(3) \end{cases}$$
2
Factor Equation (2) into Two Cases (NEVER Divide by $y$!)

From $2y(2 - \lambda) = 0$, either $y = 0$ OR $\lambda = 2$. We must solve BOTH branches:

  • Case 1 ($y = 0$): Plug $y = 0$ into constraint (3): $x^2 + 0^2 = 4 \implies x = \pm 2$. This gives two candidates: $(2, 0)$ and $(-2, 0)$. (Note: equation (1) simply yields valid multipliers $\lambda = \frac{1}{2}$ for $x = 2$ and $\lambda = \frac{3}{2}$ for $x = -2$.)
  • Case 2 ($\lambda = 2$): Plug $\lambda = 2$ into equation (1): $$x - 1 = 2x \implies -1 = x \implies x = -1$$ Now plug $x = -1$ into constraint (3): $$(-1)^2 + y^2 = 4 \implies y^2 = 3 \implies y = \pm\sqrt{3}$$ This gives two more candidates: $(-1, \sqrt{3})$ and $(-1, -\sqrt{3})$.
3
Evaluate $f(x, y) = x^2 + 2y^2 - 2x$ at All 4 Candidates
$$\begin{aligned} f(2, 0) &= 2^2 + 2(0)^2 - 2(2) = 4 - 4 = 0 && \text{(Absolute Minimum)} \\ f(-2, 0) &= (-2)^2 + 2(0)^2 - 2(-2) = 4 + 4 = 8 \\ f(-1, \sqrt{3}) &= (-1)^2 + 2(3) - 2(-1) = 1 + 6 + 2 = 9 && \text{(Absolute Maximum)} \\ f(-1, -\sqrt{3}) &= (-1)^2 + 2(3) - 2(-1) = 1 + 6 + 2 = 9 && \text{(Absolute Maximum)} \end{aligned}$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning

Notice what would have happened if you had carelessly divided $4y = 2\lambda y$ by $y$: you would have completely erased Case 1 and missed $(2, 0)$ — the true absolute minimum! Geometrically, the level ellipses $(x - 1)^2 + 2y^2 = c + 1$ centered at $(1, 0)$ grow outward until the smallest touching ellipse kisses the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 4$ internally at $(2, 0)$ and the largest touches externally at $(-1, \pm\sqrt{3})$.

Worked Example

Worked Example 2: 3D Linear Function Optimized on a Sphere

Find the absolute maximum and absolute minimum values of $f(x, y, z) = x + 2y + 2z$ subject to the sphere constraint $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 36$.
Final Answer: Absolute maximum is $18$ at $(2, 4, 4)$; absolute minimum is $-18$ at $(-2, -4, -4)$.
1
Write Out $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ in 3D

Let $g(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 36$. Since $\nabla f = \langle 1, 2, 2 \rangle$ and $\nabla g = \langle 2x, 2y, 2z \rangle$:

$$\begin{cases} 1 = 2\lambda x \quad &(1) \\ 2 = 2\lambda y \quad &(2) \\ 2 = 2\lambda z \quad &(3) \\ x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 36 \quad &(4) \end{cases}$$
2
Verify $\lambda \neq 0$ and Express $y, z$ in Terms of $x$

From equation (1), $\lambda$ cannot be $0$ (otherwise $1 = 0$, contradiction!). Similarly $x \neq 0$. Solving for $x, y, z$ in terms of $\lambda$:

$$x = \frac{1}{2\lambda}, \qquad y = \frac{1}{\lambda} = 2x, \qquad z = \frac{1}{\lambda} = 2x$$
3
Substitute into the Sphere Constraint and Evaluate $f$

Plug $y = 2x$ and $z = 2x$ into $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 36$:

$$x^2 + (2x)^2 + (2x)^2 = 36 \implies x^2 + 4x^2 + 4x^2 = 36 \implies 9x^2 = 36 \implies x^2 = 4 \implies x = \pm 2$$
  • For $x = 2$: $y = 4,\; z = 4 \implies f(2, 4, 4) = 2 + 2(4) + 2(4) = 18$ (Absolute Maximum).
  • For $x = -2$: $y = -4,\; z = -4 \implies f(-2, -4, -4) = -2 + 2(-4) + 2(-4) = -18$ (Absolute Minimum).

Physical / Geometric Meaning

The level surfaces of $f(x, y, z) = x + 2y + 2z = c$ are parallel flat planes with normal vector $\langle 1, 2, 2 \rangle$. As you slide this plane outward across the radius-$6$ sphere, it kisses the sphere at the two antipodal points where the sphere's radial vector $\langle x, y, z \rangle$ points directly along $\pm\langle 1, 2, 2 \rangle$ — namely $\pm 6 \cdot \frac{\langle 1, 2, 2 \rangle}{3} = \pm\langle 2, 4, 4 \rangle$!

Worked Example

Worked Example 3: 3D Product Optimization (The Multiply-by-$x,y,z$ Trick)

Use Lagrange multipliers to find the maximum value of $f(x, y, z) = xyz$ subject to the constraint $x + 2y + 3z = 18$ for $x > 0,\; y > 0,\; z > 0$.
Final Answer: Maximum value is $f(6, 3, 2) = 36$.
1
Write the Lagrange Equations

Let $g(x, y, z) = x + 2y + 3z = 18$. Then $\nabla f = \langle yz, xz, xy \rangle$ and $\nabla g = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle$:

$$\begin{cases} yz = \lambda \quad &(1) \\ xz = 2\lambda \quad &(2) \\ xy = 3\lambda \quad &(3) \\ x + 2y + 3z = 18 \quad &(4) \end{cases}$$
2
Use the Multiply-to-Build-$xyz$ Shortcut

Multiply equation (1) by $x$, equation (2) by $y$, and equation (3) by $z$ so every left-hand side becomes $xyz$:

$$xyz = \lambda x, \qquad xyz = 2\lambda y, \qquad xyz = 3\lambda z$$

Because $x, y, z > 0$, we know $\lambda = yz > 0$ (strictly nonzero!). Therefore we can safely divide $\lambda x = 2\lambda y = 3\lambda z$ by $\lambda$:

$$x = 2y = 3z$$
3
Substitute into the Constraint and Compute $f_{\max}$

Replace $2y$ and $3z$ by $x$ inside $x + 2y + 3z = 18$:

$$x + x + x = 18 \implies 3x = 18 \implies x = 6$$

Thus $2y = 6 \implies y = 3$, and $3z = 6 \implies z = 2$. Evaluating $f$ gives:

$$f_{\max} = (6)(3)(2) = 36$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning

Compare this 3-line Lagrange solution to the tedious expansion we did in Section 13.9 Worked Example 2! Whenever the objective is a product $x^a y^b z^c$, multiplying the Lagrange equations by $x, y, z$ immediately reveals the symmetric balance $x = 2y = 3z$ in seconds.

Worked Example 4: Optimization with Two Constraints ($\lambda$ & $\mu$)

Find the maximum and minimum values of $f(x, y, z) = x + 2y + 3z$ on the curve of intersection of the cylinder $x^2 + y^2 = 2$ and the plane $y + z = 1$.
Final Answer: Maximum value is $5$ at $(1, -1, 2)$; minimum value is $1$ at $(-1, 1, 0)$.
1
Compute All Three Gradients and Write the 5-Equation System

Set $g(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 = 2$ and $h(x, y, z) = y + z = 1$. Compute gradients:

$$\nabla f = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle, \qquad \nabla g = \langle 2x, 2y, 0 \rangle, \qquad \nabla h = \langle 0, 1, 1 \rangle$$

Write $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g + \mu \nabla h$ alongside both constraints:

$$\begin{cases} 1 = 2\lambda x \quad &(1) \\ 2 = 2\lambda y + \mu \quad &(2) \\ 3 = \mu \quad &(3) \\ x^2 + y^2 = 2 \quad &(4) \\ y + z = 1 \quad &(5) \end{cases}$$
2
Eliminate $\mu$ and $\lambda$ Cleanly

Equation (3) hands us $\mu = 3$ immediately! Substitute $\mu = 3$ into equation (2):

$$2 = 2\lambda y + 3 \implies 2\lambda y = -1$$

Now compare $2\lambda x = 1$ (from Eq. 1) and $2\lambda y = -1$. Equation (1) guarantees $\lambda \neq 0$ and $x \neq 0$. Divide $2\lambda y = -1$ by $2\lambda x = 1$:

$$\frac{2\lambda y}{2\lambda x} = \frac{-1}{1} \implies \frac{y}{x} = -1 \implies y = -x$$
3
Solve Both Constraints for $(x, y, z)$ and Evaluate $f$

Plug $y = -x$ into the cylinder equation (4):

$$x^2 + (-x)^2 = 2 \implies 2x^2 = 2 \implies x^2 = 1 \implies x = \pm 1$$
  • If $x = 1$: $y = -1$, and from plane (5) $z = 1 - y = 1 - (-1) = 2$. Point: $(1, -1, 2)$. $$f(1, -1, 2) = 1 + 2(-1) + 3(2) = 1 - 2 + 6 = 5 \quad (\text{Absolute Maximum})$$
  • If $x = -1$: $y = 1$, and from plane (5) $z = 1 - y = 1 - 1 = 0$. Point: $(-1, 1, 0)$. $$f(-1, 1, 0) = -1 + 2(1) + 3(0) = 1 \quad (\text{Absolute Minimum})$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning

Slicing a vertical cylinder of radius $\sqrt{2}$ with the tilted plane $y + z = 1$ produces a closed tilted ellipse in 3D space. The linear temperature field $f(x, y, z) = x + 2y + 3z$ achieves its hottest point ($5$) at $(1, -1, 2)$ and its coldest point ($1$) directly across the ellipse at $(-1, 1, 0)$.

Core Concept

1. Partial Integration & The "Laser-Arrow Slicing" Mental Model

Intuitive Picture: Just as a partial derivative freezes one variable like a number while differentiating the other, an iterated integral works strictly from the inside out: during the inner integral, the outer variable is frozen like an ordinary constant (e.g., $\int 6x\,dy = 6xy + C(x)$). Geometrically, finding the area of a flat 2D region $R$ means slicing $R$ into paper-thin ribbons — either vertical strips ($dy\,dx$) or horizontal strips ($dx\,dy$) — and adding up their lengths!

  • Outer limits MUST ALWAYS be constants! The final answer to a definite double integral is a number, never a formula containing $x$ or $y$.
  • Inner limits may depend ONLY on the outer variable: In $\int_a^b \int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)} f(x, y)\,dy\,dx$, the inner $y$-limits can contain $x$ (or constants), NEVER $y$.
  • How to pick $dy\,dx$ vs. $dx\,dy$ for Area: Choose the direction where every arrow enters on ONE single curve and exits on ONE single curve! Core Concept

2. Reversing the Order of Integration — The #1 KFUPM Final Exam Skill

Intuitive Picture: Imagine being handed $\displaystyle \int_0^1 \int_x^1 e^{y^2}\,dy\,dx$. If you try to evaluate the inner integral $\int e^{y^2}\,dy$ directly, you slam into a mathematical wall: $e^{y^2}$ has NO elementary antiderivative! Yet the 2D region $R$ doesn't care whether you slice it vertically or horizontally — the exact same total value comes out either way! Reversing the order turns $\int e^{y^2}\,dy$ into $\int e^{y^2}\,dx = x\,e^{y^2}$, which magically generates the extra factor of $y$ (from the new upper $x$-limit!) needed to make $u = y^2$ an instant 2-line substitution!

Instant Recognition: Classic "Impossible Inner Integral" Triggers

  • $\int e^{y^2}\,dy$, $\int e^{x^2}\,dx$, or $\int e^{x^3}\,dx$: Missing the $y$ or $x^2$ factor for $u$-substitution — reverse the order immediately!
  • $\int \sin(x^2)\,dx$, $\int \cos(y^2)\,dy$, or $\int \frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx$: Non-elementary trig integrals — reverse the order immediately!
  • $\int \sqrt{x^3 + 1}\,dx$ or $\int \sqrt{y^3 + 1}\,dy$: Missing $x^2$ or $y^2$ (usually paired with a parabolic boundary like $\sqrt{y} \le x \le 1$, which flips to $0 \le y \le x^2$ and manufactures the exact $x^2$ factor!).

The Unbreakable 4-Step Order-Reversal Algorithm

  1. Step 1 — Decode the Inequalities: Read the outer limits (constants) and inner limits (curves) straight off the given integral. For example, $\int_0^1 \int_{\sqrt{y}}^1 f\,dx\,dy$ means $0 \le y \le 1$ and $\sqrt{y} \le x \le 1$.
  2. Step 2 — Sketch the Region $R$ & Label Every Vertex: Draw every boundary curve cleanly in the $xy$-plane (e.g., $x = \sqrt{y} \iff y = x^2$ for $x \ge 0$, together with $x = 1$ and $y = 0$). Shade the exact region trapped between the entry curve and exit curve!
  3. Step 3 — Re-Slice in the Opposite Direction: Draw a single representative arrow in the new inner direction (vertical UP for $dy\,dx$, or horizontal RIGHT for $dx\,dy$). Read off:
    • New outer limits: The full constant span of the outer variable across $R$ (e.g., $0 \le x \le 1$).
    • New inner limits: Solve the entry and exit boundary equations for the new inner variable (e.g., bottom $y = 0$ to top $y = x^2$).
  4. Step 4 — Evaluate Inside-Out: The inner integral now treats the impossible integrand as a constant, generating the exact factor needed for $u$-substitution in the outer integral!

KFUPM Exam Traps to Avoid in Section 14.1

  • Trap 1 — Blindly Swapping the Integral Signs: Writing $\int_0^1 \int_x^1 e^{y^2}\,dy\,dx = \int_x^1 \int_0^1 e^{y^2}\,dx\,dy$ is catastrophically WRONG! You can ONLY swap limits directly when the region is a pure rectangle with constant bounds on all four sides. For ANY variable bound, you MUST sketch $R$ first!
  • Trap 2 — Shading the Wrong Half of a Triangle: Students confuse $x \le y \le 1$ (the upper-left triangle above $y = x$) with $0 \le y \le x$ (the lower-right triangle below $y = x$). Always pick one test point (like $(0.2, 0.8)$) to confirm it satisfies the inequalities!
  • Trap 3 — Forgetting the Chain-Rule Constant in $u$-Substitution: After reversing the order to get $\int_0^1 y e^{y^2}\,dy$, do not drop the $\frac{1}{2}$ from $u = y^2 \implies du = 2y\,dy \implies y\,dy = \frac{1}{2}\,du$.
Worked Example

Worked Example 1: Evaluating an Iterated Integral with Variable Bounds

Evaluate the iterated integral $\displaystyle \int_0^2 \int_x^{2x} (6x + 2y)\,dy\,dx$.
Final Answer: $24$.
1
Evaluate the Inner $y$-Integral (Holding $x$ Completely Constant)

Integrate $(6x + 2y)$ with respect to $y$. Because $6x$ is a constant with respect to $y$, its antiderivative is $6xy$; the antiderivative of $2y$ is $y^2$:

$$\begin{aligned} \int_x^{2x} (6x + 2y)\,dy &= \Big[\, 6xy + y^2 \,\Big]_{y = x}^{y = 2x} \\ &= \big(6x(2x) + (2x)^2\big) - \big(6x(x) + x^2\big) \\ &= (12x^2 + 4x^2) - (6x^2 + x^2) \\ &= 16x^2 - 7x^2 = 9x^2 \end{aligned}$$

Notice: After plugging in the $y$-bounds, every single $y$ vanishes and only a clean function of $x$ remains!

2
Evaluate the Outer $x$-Integral

Integrate the result $9x^2$ over the constant outer bounds $0 \le x \le 2$:

$$\int_0^2 9x^2\,dx = \Big[\, 3x^3 \,\Big]_0^2 = 3(2^3) - 3(0^3) = 3(8) = 24$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning

The region $R$ is the triangle bounded by $y = x$, $y = 2x$, and the vertical wall $x = 2$ (vertices $(0,0)$, $(2,2)$, $(2,4)$). The value $24$ represents the exact 3D volume trapped above that triangle and beneath the tilted roof plane $z = 6x + 2y$.

Worked Example

Worked Example 2: Area in the Plane — Why Horizontal Slicing ($dx\,dy$) Beats Vertical ($dy\,dx$)

Find the area of the region $R$ bounded by the sideways parabola $x = y^2$ and the line $y = x - 2$. Set up the area integral in the smarter order and evaluate it.
Final Answer: $A = \dfrac{9}{2}$ square units (using a single horizontal integral $\displaystyle \int_{-1}^{2} \int_{y^2}^{y + 2} 1\,dx\,dy$).
1
Find All Intersection Points of the Boundary Curves

Rewrite the line as $x = y + 2$ and set it equal to the parabola $x = y^2$:

$$y^2 = y + 2 \implies y^2 - y - 2 = 0 \implies (y - 2)(y + 1) = 0$$

This gives two intersection heights:

  • $y = -1 \implies x = (-1)^2 = 1 \implies$ bottom intersection $(1, -1)$.
  • $y = 2 \implies x = 2^2 = 4 \implies$ top intersection $(4, 2)$.
2
Compare Vertical ($dy\,dx$) vs. Horizontal ($dx\,dy$) Slicing

If we slice vertically ($dy\,dx$): From $x = 0$ to $x = 1$, a vertical arrow goes from the lower parabola $y = -\sqrt{x}$ to the upper parabola $y = +\sqrt{x}$. At $x = 1$, the bottom boundary suddenly switches to the line $y = x - 2$! That forces us to split the region into TWO separate integrals:

$$A = \int_0^1 \int_{-\sqrt{x}}^{\sqrt{x}} 1\,dy\,dx + \int_1^4 \int_{x - 2}^{\sqrt{x}} 1\,dy\,dx$$

If we slice horizontally ($dx\,dy$): Between $y = -1$ and $y = 2$, EVERY single horizontal arrow enters on the left at the parabola $x = y^2$ and exits on the right at the line $x = y + 2$! Only ONE clean integral with zero square roots:

$$A = \int_{-1}^{2} \int_{y^2}^{y + 2} 1\,dx\,dy$$
3
Evaluate the Single Horizontal Iterated Integral
$$\begin{aligned} A &= \int_{-1}^{2} \Big[\, x \,\Big]_{x = y^2}^{x = y + 2}\,dy = \int_{-1}^{2} (y + 2 - y^2)\,dy \\ &= \left[\, \frac{y^2}{2} + 2y - \frac{y^3}{3} \,\right]_{-1}^{2} \\ &= \left(\frac{4}{2} + 4 - \frac{8}{3}\right) - \left(\frac{1}{2} - 2 - \frac{-1}{3}\right) \\ &= \left(6 - \frac{8}{3}\right) - \left(\frac{1}{2} - 2 + \frac{1}{3}\right) \\ &= \frac{10}{3} - \left(-\frac{7}{6}\right) = \frac{20}{6} + \frac{7}{6} = \frac{27}{6} = \frac{9}{2} \end{aligned}$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning

Choosing the right slicing orientation cut our workload in half and eliminated every square root! Physically, the inner integral $\int_{y^2}^{y+2} 1\,dx = (y + 2) - y^2$ measures the exact horizontal width of a thin strip at height $y$, and the outer integral sums those horizontal ribbon widths from $y = -1$ up to $y = 2$ to get the total enclosed area $4.5$.

Worked Example

Worked Example 3: Reversing the Order of Integration to Unlock $\int e^{y^2}\,dy$

Evaluate the iterated integral $\displaystyle \int_0^1 \int_x^1 e^{y^2}\,dy\,dx$ by reversing the order of integration.
Final Answer: $\dfrac{e - 1}{2}$.
1
Spot the Brick Wall & Extract the Region Inequalities

The inner integral $\int_x^1 e^{y^2}\,dy$ has no elementary antiderivative, so we must reverse the order from $dy\,dx$ to $dx\,dy$. Read the exact region $R$ from the given limits:

$$R:\quad 0 \le x \le 1 \quad \text{and} \quad x \le y \le 1$$
2
Identify the Region Geometry and Re-Slice Horizontally ($dx\,dy$)

Sketch the bounding lines: bottom-right boundary is the diagonal $y = x$, top boundary is the horizontal line $y = 1$, and left boundary is the vertical $y$-axis $x = 0$. This forms the upper-left triangle with vertices $(0, 0)$, $(0, 1)$, and $(1, 1)$. (Test point check: $(0.2, 0.8)$ satisfies $0 \le 0.2 \le 1$ and $0.2 \le 0.8 \le 1$ — yes!)

Now fire horizontal arrows straight RIGHT through this triangle:

  • Lowest to highest $y$-value across the triangle: $0 \le y \le 1$ (new outer constant bounds).
  • Each horizontal arrow enters at the left wall $x = 0$ and exits at the diagonal line $x = y$: $0 \le x \le y$ (new inner bounds).
3
Evaluate the Reversed Integral with $u$-Substitution

Rewrite the integral in the $dx\,dy$ order. During the inner $x$-integral, $e^{y^2}$ is a pure constant!

$$\int_0^1 \int_0^y e^{y^2}\,dx\,dy = \int_0^1 \Big[\, x\,e^{y^2} \,\Big]_{x = 0}^{x = y}\,dy = \int_0^1 y\,e^{y^2}\,dy$$

Now $u$-substitution works effortlessly! Let $u = y^2 \implies du = 2y\,dy \implies y\,dy = \dfrac{1}{2}\,du$. Change the limits: when $y = 0$, $u = 0$; when $y = 1$, $u = 1$:

$$\int_0^1 y\,e^{y^2}\,dy = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 e^u\,du = \frac{1}{2}\Big[\, e^u \,\Big]_0^1 = \frac{e^1 - e^0}{2} = \frac{e - 1}{2}$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning

Because the height $z = e^{y^2}$ does not depend on $x$ at all, every horizontal cross-section across the triangle has a completely flat ceiling of height $e^{y^2}$ and width $y$ — so its slice area is simply $\text{width} \times \text{height} = y\,e^{y^2}$! Integrating those cross-sectional areas from $y = 0$ to $y = 1$ gives the exact volume $\frac{e - 1}{2} \approx 0.859$.

Evaluate the iterated integral $\displaystyle \int_0^{2\sqrt{\pi}} \int_{y/2}^{\sqrt{\pi}} \sin(x^2)\,dx\,dy$ by reversing the order of integration.
Final Answer: $2$.

Decode the Given $dx\,dy$ Inequalities

Sketch the Triangle and Re-Slice Vertically ($dy\,dx$)

Physical / Geometric Meaning

Because $\sin(x^2) \ge 0$ throughout $0 \le x \le \sqrt{\pi}$, the reversed vertical slicing reveals that the surface $z = \sin(x^2)$ rises like a smooth wave arch over the triangle, enclosing a clean total volume of exactly $2$ cubic units — a result that looked completely unreachable in the original $dx\,dy$ order!

Core Intuition

1. What Is a Double Integral? (Stacking Tiny Rectangular Columns)

In Calculus I, a single integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ cuts a 2D region into paper-thin vertical rectangles of width $dx$ and height $f(x)$, then adds up their areas. A double integral $\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA$ is the exact 3D upgrade: imagine building a mountain range out of millions of ultra-thin square LEGO towers standing on a flat floor region $R$.

Step-by-Step Geometric Breakdown

  1. The Footprint ($R$): Start with a closed, bounded 2D region $R$ lying flat on the $xy$-plane. Chop $R$ into a fine grid of tiny rectangles. Each tiny tile has width $\Delta x$ and depth $\Delta y$, so its base area is $\Delta A = \Delta x\,\Delta y$.
  2. One Tiny Column ($f(x,y)\,dA$): Above each tile at point $(x,y)$, erect a thin vertical prism straight up to the surface $z = f(x,y)$. Its volume is: $$dV = (\text{height}) \times (\text{base area}) = f(x,y)\,dA$$
  3. Summing All Columns (The Double Integral): Add up the volumes of all the towers across $R$ and shrink the tile size to zero ($\Delta x, \Delta y \to 0$): $$\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \lim_{\|\Delta\| \to 0} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i, y_i)\,\Delta A_i$$

Signed Volume vs. True Geometric Volume

  • When $f(x,y) \ge 0$ everywhere on $R$: Every column rises above the $xy$-plane, so $\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA$ equals the exact volume of the solid sitting beneath the roof $z = f(x,y)$ and directly above the floor $R$.
  • When $f(x,y)$ changes sign on $R$: Columns below the $xy$-plane contribute negative values. Thus $\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA$ computes the net signed volume (volume above the $xy$-plane minus volume below).
  • Special Case $f(x,y) = 1$: A solid with constant height $1$ has numerical volume equal to its base area! That gives the super-useful area formula: $$\text{Area}(R) = \iint_R 1\,dA$$
Method & Strategy

2. Volume Between Two Surfaces & Finding the Shadow $R$

Most KFUPM exam volume problems do not sit on a flat floor $z = 0$ — they are trapped between an upper roof surface $z = z_{\text{top}}(x,y)$ and a lower floor surface $z = z_{\text{bottom}}(x,y)$. Instead of memorizing special cases, use one universal formula.

Volume Trapped Between Two Surfaces

If a solid is bounded above by $z = z_{\text{top}}(x,y)$ and below by $z = z_{\text{bottom}}(x,y)$ over a 2D footprint $R$ in the $xy$-plane, then each vertical column has height $(z_{\text{top}} - z_{\text{bottom}})$ and the total volume is:

$$V = \iint_R \left(z_{\text{top}}(x,y) - z_{\text{bottom}}(x,y)\right) dA$$

How to Find the Shadow (Projection) $R$ on the $xy$-Plane

Students rarely get stuck on the integration itself — they get stuck figuring out the 2D region $R$! Imagine shining a bright overhead floodlight straight down from $z = +\infty$. The shadow cast onto the $xy$-plane is $R$. There are two main ways $R$ is determined:

  • Case A — The Roof and Floor Meet on Their Own (No Vertical Walls Given): Set the two surfaces equal: $z_{\text{top}}(x,y) = z_{\text{bottom}}(x,y)$. Eliminating $z$ gives an equation purely in $x$ and $y$ — that curve is the exact outer rim of the shadow $R$!
    • Example: Between $z = 4 - x^2 - y^2$ and $z = 4 - 2x$, setting them equal gives $4 - x^2 - y^2 = 4 - 2x \implies x^2 - 2x + y^2 = 0 \implies (x-1)^2 + y^2 = 1$, a disk of radius $1$ centered at $(1,0)$.
  • Case B — Explicitly Bounded by Vertical Walls or the First Octant: The problem gives vertical planes/cylinders (like $y = x$, $y = 1$, $x = 0$) or says "in the first octant" ($x \ge 0, y \ge 0, z \ge 0$). If the solid hits the $xy$-plane, set $z = 0$ in the roof equation to see where the roof touches the floor.

Turning $\iint_R$ into an Iterated Integral (Fubini's Theorem)

Once you sketch the 2D shadow $R$, choose the sweeping direction that avoids splitting $R$ into pieces:

  • Vertical Slices ($dy\,dx$ — Type I): Fix an $x$ between constants $a \le x \le b$. Shoot a vertical arrow upward through $R$: it enters at $y = g_1(x)$ (bottom curve) and exits at $y = g_2(x)$ (top curve): $$\iint_R h(x,y)\,dA = \int_a^b \int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)} h(x,y)\,dy\,dx$$
  • Horizontal Slices ($dx\,dy$ — Type II): Fix a $y$ between constants $c \le y \le d$. Shoot a horizontal arrow rightward through $R$: it enters at $x = h_1(y)$ (left curve) and exits at $x = h_2(y)$ (right curve): $$\iint_R h(x,y)\,dA = \int_c^d \int_{h_1(y)}^{h_2(y)} h(x,y)\,dx\,dy$$
High-Yield Application

3. Average Value of a Function over a Region $R$

Picture a sandbox shaped like region $R$ filled with an uneven pile of wet sand whose height at $(x,y)$ is $f(x,y)$. Now shake the sandbox until the sand settles completely flat at one uniform height $f_{\text{avg}}$. Since no sand was added or lost, the volume of the flat slab must equal the volume of the original pile!

Average Value Formula

Because $\text{Flat Volume} = f_{\text{avg}} \cdot \text{Area}(R) = \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA$, solving for $f_{\text{avg}}$ gives:

$$f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{\text{Area}(R)} \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA, \qquad \text{where } \text{Area}(R) = \iint_R 1\,dA$$

Time-Saving Exam Tip: If $R$ is a basic geometric shape (rectangle, triangle, circle, or semicircle), compute $\text{Area}(R)$ directly using elementary geometry ($\text{base}\times\text{height}$, $\frac{1}{2}bh$, or $\pi r^2$) instead of wasting time integrating $1$!

KFUPM Exam Traps in Section 14.2
  • Trap 1 — Forgetting to Divide by $\text{Area}(R)$: On Average Value questions, the raw double integral $\iint_R f\,dA$ is almost ALWAYS planted as one of the wrong multiple-choice distractors! Double-check that you divided by $\text{Area}(R)$ (and didn't drop the $\frac{1}{2}$ on a triangle!).
  • Trap 2 — Getting $z_{\text{top}} - z_{\text{bottom}}$ Backwards: Never guess which surface is higher just by eyeballing the formulas. Pick ONE easy interior point $(x_0, y_0)$ inside $R$ and plug it into both surfaces — the larger $z$-value is guaranteed to be $z_{\text{top}}$.
  • Trap 3 — Misreading Shifted Circle Bounds: After completing the square on $x^2 - 2x + y^2 = 0 \implies (x-1)^2 + y^2 = 1$, the center is $(1,0)$ and radius is $1$, so $x$ runs over $[0, 2]$ (NOT $[-1, 1]$!) and $y$ runs over $\left[-\sqrt{1-(x-1)^2},\; \sqrt{1-(x-1)^2}\right]$.

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Identify the Roof, Floor, and 2D Shadow $R$

"First octant" means $x \ge 0$, $y \ge 0$, and $z \ge 0$. In the $xy$-plane, the region bounded by $x = 0$, $y = x$, and $y = 1$ is the right triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(0,1)$, and $(1,1)$.

Check that the surface stays above the floor on this triangle: since $0 \le x \le 1$ and $0 \le y \le 1$, we have $0 \le xy \le 1$, so $z = 8(1 - xy) \ge 0$ everywhere on $R$. Thus:

$$z_{\text{top}} = 8(1 - xy), \qquad z_{\text{bottom}} = 0$$
2
Set Up the Iterated Integral Limits

Using horizontal slices ($dx\,dy$): $y$ runs from $0$ to $1$. For a fixed $y$, a horizontal arrow enters at the left edge $x = 0$ and exits at the slanted line $x = y$:

$$V = \int_0^1 \int_0^y 8(1 - xy)\,dx\,dy$$
3
Evaluate the Inner $x$-Integral (Hold $y$ Constant)

Factor out the constant $8$ and integrate term by term with respect to $x$:

$$\int_0^y (1 - xy)\,dx = \left[x - \frac{1}{2}x^2 y\right]_{x=0}^{x=y} = \left(y - \frac{1}{2}y^3\right) - (0 - 0) = y - \frac{1}{2}y^3$$
4
Evaluate the Outer $y$-Integral
$$V = 8 \int_0^1 \left(y - \frac{1}{2}y^3\right) dy = 8\left[\frac{y^2}{2} - \frac{y^4}{8}\right]_0^1 = 8\left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{8}\right) = 8\left(\frac{3}{8}\right) = 3$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: The triangular base has area $\frac{1}{2}(1)(1) = 0.5$. The roof stands at height $z = 8$ along the $y$-axis ($x = 0$) and slopes down to touch $z = 0$ at the corner $(1,1)$. A base of area $0.5$ with an average roof height of $6$ yields a volume of $0.5 \times 6 = 3$ cubic units.

Worked Example 2: Setting Up & Evaluating Volume Between Two Surfaces

Problem Statement

Set up an iterated double integral in rectangular coordinates for the volume of the solid region bounded by the graphs of $z = 4 - x^2 - y^2$ and $z = 4 - 2x$, and then compute its exact value.

Final Answer

Final Answer: $$V = \int_0^2 \int_{-\sqrt{1 - (x - 1)^2}}^{\sqrt{1 - (x - 1)^2}} (2x - x^2 - y^2)\,dy\,dx = \mathbf{\frac{\pi}{2}}$$

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Find the Shadow $R$ by Intersecting the Two Surfaces

No vertical walls are mentioned, so the solid closes where the downward-opening paraboloid meets the slanted plane. Set $z_1 = z_2$:

$$4 - x^2 - y^2 = 4 - 2x \implies x^2 - 2x + y^2 = 0$$

Complete the square in $x$ by adding $1$ to both sides:

$$(x^2 - 2x + 1) + y^2 = 1 \implies (x - 1)^2 + y^2 = 1$$

The shadow $R$ is the circular disk centered at $(1, 0)$ with radius $1$. Therefore, $x$ spans from $1 - 1 = 0$ to $1 + 1 = 2$, and for each $x$, solving for $y$ gives:

$$-\sqrt{1 - (x - 1)^2} \le y \le \sqrt{1 - (x - 1)^2}$$
2
Test an Interior Point to Identify Roof vs. Floor

Pick the center of the disk $(x, y) = (1, 0)$:

  • Paraboloid: $z = 4 - 1^2 - 0^2 = 3$ (higher $\implies$ Roof).
  • Plane: $z = 4 - 2(1) = 2$ (lower $\implies$ Floor).

Compute the vertical column height $z_{\text{top}} - z_{\text{bottom}}$:

$$z_{\text{top}} - z_{\text{bottom}} = (4 - x^2 - y^2) - (4 - 2x) = 2x - x^2 - y^2$$

This gives the exact rectangular setup tested on KFUPM exams:

$$V = \int_0^2 \int_{-\sqrt{1 - (x - 1)^2}}^{\sqrt{1 - (x - 1)^2}} (2x - x^2 - y^2)\,dy\,dx$$
3
Evaluate via Centering Substitution $u = x - 1$

Notice that $2x - x^2 = 1 - (x - 1)^2$. Letting $u = x - 1$ ($du = dx$, $u \in [-1, 1]$) simplifies the integrand to $(1 - u^2) - y^2$:

$$\int_{-\sqrt{1 - u^2}}^{\sqrt{1 - u^2}} \left[(1 - u^2) - y^2\right] dy = 2\left[(1 - u^2)y - \frac{y^3}{3}\right]_0^{\sqrt{1 - u^2}} = 2\left((1 - u^2)^{3/2} - \frac{1}{3}(1 - u^2)^{3/2}\right) = \frac{4}{3}(1 - u^2)^{3/2}$$
4
Finish the Outer Integral with Trig Substitution

Set $u = \sin t$, $du = \cos t\,dt$, with $t \in [-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}]$, and use $\cos^2 t = \frac{1 + \cos(2t)}{2}$ twice:

$$\begin{aligned} V &= \frac{4}{3} \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \cos^4 t\,dt = \frac{4}{3} \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \left(\frac{1 + \cos 2t}{2}\right)^2 dt \\ &= \frac{1}{3} \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \left(1 + 2\cos 2t + \frac{1 + \cos 4t}{2}\right) dt \\ &= \frac{1}{3} \left[\frac{3}{2}t + \sin 2t + \frac{1}{8}\sin 4t\right]_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} = \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}\right) = \frac{\pi}{2} \end{aligned}$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: The solid is a paraboloid cap sliced diagonally by a plane. Its circular shadow has area $\pi(1)^2 = \pi$, its maximum thickness (at the center) is $3 - 2 = 1$, and its exact volume is $\frac{1}{2}(\text{base area})(\text{max height}) = \frac{\pi}{2}$ — a classic property of every circular paraboloid cap! (In Topic 07, we will see how polar coordinates solve integrals over disks like this in 3 lines.)

Worked Example 3: Average Value Over a Triangular Region

Problem Statement

Let $f(x, y) = 2xy$ and let $R$ be the triangular region with vertices $(0, 0)$, $(-3, -1)$, and $(0, -1)$. Find the average value of $f$ over $R$.

Final Answer

Final Answer: $f_{\text{avg}} = \mathbf{\frac{3}{2}}$

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Sketch the Triangle and Find Its Area

Plot the three vertices in Quadrant III:

  • Vertical right leg from $(0, -1)$ to $(0, 0)$ along $x = 0$ (length $= 1$).
  • Horizontal bottom leg from $(-3, -1)$ to $(0, -1)$ along $y = -1$ (length $= 3$).
  • Slanted hypotenuse from $(-3, -1)$ to $(0, 0)$: slope is $m = \frac{0 - (-1)}{0 - (-3)} = \frac{1}{3}$, so $y = \frac{1}{3}x \iff x = 3y$.

Because it is a right triangle, its area is immediate:

$$\text{Area}(R) = \frac{1}{2}(\text{base})(\text{height}) = \frac{1}{2}(3)(1) = \frac{3}{2}$$
2
Choose Horizontal Slices ($dx\,dy$) to Avoid Fractions

Notice $y$ ranges from $-1$ to $0$. For any fixed $y \in [-1, 0]$, $3y$ is negative and sits to the left of $x = 0$. So $x$ runs from the left slanted line $x = 3y$ to the right vertical line $x = 0$:

$$\iint_R 2xy\,dA = \int_{-1}^0 \int_{3y}^0 2xy\,dx\,dy$$
3
Evaluate the Inner and Outer Integrals

Inner integral (with respect to $x$, holding $y$ fixed):

$$\int_{3y}^0 2xy\,dx = \left[x^2 y\right]_{x=3y}^{x=0} = 0 - (3y)^2 y = -9y^3$$

Outer integral (with respect to $y$):

$$\int_{-1}^0 (-9y^3)\,dy = \left[-\frac{9}{4}y^4\right]_{-1}^0 = 0 - \left(-\frac{9}{4}(-1)^4\right) = \frac{9}{4}$$
4
Divide by $\text{Area}(R)$
$$f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{\text{Area}(R)} \iint_R 2xy\,dA = \frac{9/4}{3/2} = \frac{9}{4} \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{3}{2}$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: Inside Quadrant III, both $x \le 0$ and $y \le 0$, so their product $2xy$ is always non-negative, ranging from $0$ along the edges up to $2(-3)(-1) = 6$ at the corner $(-3, -1)$. An average height of $\frac{3}{2} = 1.5$ sits right inside $[0, 6]$ — an instant sanity check!

Worked Example 4: Solving for an Unknown Parameter $a$ from Average Value

Problem Statement

Let $f(x, y) = a x^2 + a y^2$, where $a > 0$, and let $R = \{(x, y) : 0 \le x \le a,\; 0 \le y \le a\}$. If the average value of $f$ over $R$ is $18$, find the value of $a$.

Final Answer

Final Answer: $a = \mathbf{3}$

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Find the Area of the Square $R$

The region $R = [0, a] \times [0, a]$ is a square of side length $a$, so:

$$\text{Area}(R) = a \cdot a = a^2$$
2
Compute $\iint_R (ax^2 + ay^2)\,dA$

Integrate with respect to $y$ first, then $x$:

$$\begin{aligned} \int_0^a \int_0^a (ax^2 + ay^2)\,dy\,dx &= \int_0^a \left[ax^2 y + \frac{a}{3}y^3\right]_{y=0}^{y=a} dx \\ &= \int_0^a \left(a^2 x^2 + \frac{a^4}{3}\right) dx \\ &= \left[\frac{a^2 x^3}{3} + \frac{a^4}{3}x\right]_0^a = \frac{a^5}{3} + \frac{a^5}{3} = \frac{2}{3}a^5 \end{aligned}$$
3
Set $f_{\text{avg}} = 18$ and Solve for $a$
$$f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{\text{Area}(R)} \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \frac{\frac{2}{3}a^5}{a^2} = \frac{2}{3}a^3$$ $$\frac{2}{3}a^3 = 18 \implies a^3 = 18 \cdot \frac{3}{2} = 27 \implies a = 3$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: By symmetry on the square $[0, a]^2$, the average of $x^2$ equals $\frac{1}{a}\int_0^a x^2\,dx = \frac{a^2}{3}$ and the average of $y^2$ is also $\frac{a^2}{3}$. Thus the average of $a(x^2 + y^2)$ is $a\left(\frac{a^2}{3} + \frac{a^2}{3}\right) = \frac{2}{3}a^3$. Scaling $a = 3$ produces an average surface height of $\frac{2}{3}(27) = 18$.

Core Intuition

1. Why Circular Regions Scream "Use Polar Coordinates!"

Try fitting square tiles inside a round pizza — the edges turn into ugly jagged square roots like $y = \pm\sqrt{9 - x^2}$. Switch to polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ and that messy circle becomes a dead-simple "polar rectangle": $0 \le r \le 3$ and $0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$!

The Two Signals to Switch to Polar Immediately

  • Signal 1 — The Region $R$ Is Round: Disks ($x^2 + y^2 \le a^2$), rings/annuli ($a^2 \le x^2 + y^2 \le b^2$), semicircles, quarter-circles, pie wedges, or shifted circles ($x^2 + y^2 = 2ax$ or $2ay$).
  • Signal 2 — The Integrand Contains $x^2 + y^2$: Expressions like $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, $(x^2 + y^2)^{3/2}$, $e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}$, $\sin(x^2 + y^2)$, or $\frac{1}{1 + x^2 + y^2}$ collapse instantly via $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$!

Conversion Dictionary:

$$x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad x^2 + y^2 = r^2, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}$$
Deep Derivation

2. WHY Does the Extra $r$ Appear in $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$?

Every student remembers being warned: "Don't forget the $r$!" — yet many still forget it under exam pressure because they treat it like a random magic tax. Once you see the geometry, you will NEVER forget it again.

Three Ways to See Why $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$ (NOT $dr\,d\theta$)

  1. The Pizza-Crust (Polar Wedge) Picture: Instead of cutting $R$ with vertical and horizontal lines, chop it with concentric circles (spaced by $\Delta r$) and rays from the origin (spaced by $\Delta\theta$). One tiny polar tile is a curved crust piece:
    • Its radial thickness (inner rim to outer rim) is $\Delta r$.
    • Its curved arc width is NOT $\Delta\theta$! By the arc-length formula $s = r\theta$, a tile sitting at radius $r$ has arc width $r\,\Delta\theta$. Notice that tiles near the origin ($r \approx 0$) are tiny and pinched, while tiles far out (large $r$) are wide!
    • Since the tiny crust is nearly a rectangle, its area is: $$\Delta A \approx (\text{thickness}) \times (\text{arc width}) = (\Delta r)(r\,\Delta\theta) = r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta$$
  2. Exact Sector-Subtraction Proof: Area of a circular sector is $\frac{1}{2}(\text{radius})^2(\text{angle})$. Subtracting the inner sector of radius $r$ from the outer sector of radius $r + \Delta r$: $$\Delta A = \frac{1}{2}(r + \Delta r)^2 \Delta\theta - \frac{1}{2}r^2 \Delta\theta = \frac{1}{2}\left(2r\,\Delta r + (\Delta r)^2\right)\Delta\theta = r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta + \frac{1}{2}(\Delta r)^2\Delta\theta$$ As $\Delta r \to 0$, the $(\Delta r)^2$ term vanishes faster than $\Delta r$, leaving $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$.
  3. Units (Dimensional) Sanity Check: $r$ is measured in meters ($\text{m}$), $dr$ is in meters ($\text{m}$), and $d\theta$ is in radians (dimensionless!). Therefore $dr\,d\theta$ has units of length ($\text{m}$), NOT area! Multiplying by $r$ gives $r\,dr\,d\theta$ with units of $\text{m}^2$ — true area!

Change of Variables to Polar Coordinates

If $R$ is described in polar coordinates by $\alpha \le \theta \le \beta$ and $h_1(\theta) \le r \le h_2(\theta)$ (with $0 \le \beta - \alpha \le 2\pi$), then:

$$\iint_R f(x, y)\,dA = \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} \int_{h_1(\theta)}^{h_2(\theta)} f(r\cos\theta,\, r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta$$

Bonus Shortcut: When the limits of $r$ and $\theta$ are all constants AND the integrand factors as $g(r)\,h(\theta)$, split the double integral into a product of two 1D integrals: $$\int_{\alpha}^{\beta} \int_a^b g(r)\,h(\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta = \left(\int_{\alpha}^{\beta} h(\theta)\,d\theta\right)\left(\int_a^b g(r)\,r\,dr\right)$$

Worked Example 1: Converting a Cartesian Wedge Integral to Polar

Problem Statement

Evaluate the iterated integral by converting to polar coordinates:

$$\int_0^{3/\sqrt{2}} \int_y^{\sqrt{9 - y^2}} \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\,dx\,dy$$
Final Answer

Final Answer: $\mathbf{\frac{9\pi}{4}}$

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Decode the Cartesian Inequalities & Sketch the Region $R$

Read the bounds from outside in:

$$0 \le y \le \frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad y \le x \le \sqrt{9 - y^2}$$
  • Right boundary: $x = \sqrt{9 - y^2} \implies x^2 + y^2 = 9$ (right half of the circle of radius $3$).
  • Left boundary: $x = y$ (the $45^\circ$ ray $\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$).
  • Intersection of $x = y$ and $x^2 + y^2 = 9$: $2y^2 = 9 \implies y = \frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}$ (exact match with the outer upper limit!).

Since $y \ge 0$ and $x \ge y$, the region is the circular pie wedge in Quadrant I lying below $y = x$ and above the $+x$-axis.

2
Translate Bounds and Integrand to Polar
  • Angle sweep: from the $+x$-axis ($\theta = 0$) up to $y = x$ ($\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$).
  • Radius sweep: from the origin ($r = 0$) out to the circle ($r = 3$).
  • Integrand + Jacobian: $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\,dx\,dy = r \cdot (r\,dr\,d\theta) = r^2\,dr\,d\theta$.
$$I = \int_0^{\pi/4} \int_0^3 r^2\,dr\,d\theta$$
3
Evaluate the Separable Polar Integral
$$I = \left(\int_0^{\pi/4} 1\,d\theta\right)\left(\int_0^3 r^2\,dr\right) = \left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\left[\frac{r^3}{3}\right]_0^3 = \frac{\pi}{4} \cdot \frac{27}{3} = \frac{9\pi}{4}$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: Since $z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ is an upward-opening cone, this integral computes the volume of the region under the cone $z = r$ and above an eighth-disk of radius $3$. Notice a full cylinder of radius $3$ and height $3$ minus the cone inside it has volume $\pi r^2 h - \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \frac{2}{3}\pi(27) = 18\pi$, and $\frac{1}{8}$ of $18\pi$ is $\frac{18\pi}{8} = \frac{9\pi}{4}$!

Worked Example 2: Evaluating a Linear Integrand Over a Quarter-Disk

Problem Statement

If $R = \{(x, y) : x^2 + y^2 \le 9,\; x \ge 0,\; y \ge 0\}$, evaluate $\displaystyle \iint_R (x + y)\,dA$.

Final Answer

Final Answer: $\mathbf{18}$

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Write the Quarter-Disk in Polar Coordinates

$x^2 + y^2 \le 9$ is the disk of radius $3$. Restricting to $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ keeps only Quadrant I:

$$0 \le r \le 3, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$$
2
Substitute $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, and $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$
$$\iint_R (x + y)\,dA = \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^3 (r\cos\theta + r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta = \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^3 r^2(\cos\theta + \sin\theta)\,dr\,d\theta$$
3
Factor into Two Independent 1D Integrals and Solve
$$\begin{aligned} I &= \left(\int_0^{\pi/2} (\cos\theta + \sin\theta)\,d\theta\right)\left(\int_0^3 r^2\,dr\right) \\ &= \Big[\sin\theta - \cos\theta\Big]_0^{\pi/2} \cdot \left[\frac{r^3}{3}\right]_0^3 \\ &= \big((1 - 0) - (0 - 1)\big) \cdot \left(\frac{27}{3} - 0\right) = (2)(9) = 18 \end{aligned}$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: Surprisingly, no $\pi$ appears in the final answer! Because $\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\,d\theta = 1$ and $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin\theta\,d\theta = 1$, the volume of the wedge under the slanted plane $z = x + y$ above the quarter-disk of radius $3$ is an exact integer: $18$ cubic units.

Worked Example 3: Volume Trapped Between Two Paraboloids

Problem Statement

Find the volume of the solid bounded above by $z = 12 - x^2 - y^2$ and below by $z = 2x^2 + 2y^2$.

Final Answer

Final Answer: $V = \mathbf{24\pi}$

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Find the Intersection Curve to Determine the Shadow $R$

Set the upper and lower paraboloids equal:

$$12 - x^2 - y^2 = 2x^2 + 2y^2 \implies 3(x^2 + y^2) = 12 \implies x^2 + y^2 = 4$$

The shadow $R$ on the $xy$-plane is the full disk of radius $2$: $0 \le r \le 2$ and $0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$.

2
Express $(z_{\text{top}} - z_{\text{bottom}})$ in Polar Form

In polar coordinates, $z_{\text{top}} = 12 - r^2$ and $z_{\text{bottom}} = 2r^2$. Thus:

$$z_{\text{top}} - z_{\text{bottom}} = (12 - r^2) - 2r^2 = 12 - 3r^2$$
3
Integrate with $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$
$$\begin{aligned} V &= \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^2 (12 - 3r^2)\,r\,dr\,d\theta \\ &= \left(\int_0^{2\pi} d\theta\right)\left(\int_0^2 (12r - 3r^3)\,dr\right) \\ &= 2\pi \left[6r^2 - \frac{3}{4}r^4\right]_0^2 = 2\pi \left(6(4) - \frac{3}{4}(16)\right) = 2\pi(24 - 12) = 24\pi \end{aligned}$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: The solid resembles a symmetrical spinning top (egg-shaped cavity) extending vertically from $z = 0$ (bottom tip) to $z = 12$ (top tip) with circular cross-section radius $2$ at its widest seam $z = 8$. Its volume $24\pi$ is exactly half of the enclosing cylinder $\pi(2^2)(12) = 48\pi$!

Worked Example 4: Hemisphere Pierced by a Shifted Cylinder ($r = 2\cos\theta$)

Problem Statement

Find the volume of the solid lying above the $xy$-plane, below the hemisphere $z = \sqrt{4 - x^2 - y^2}$, and inside the shifted cylinder $x^2 + y^2 = 2x$.

Final Answer

Final Answer: $V = \mathbf{\frac{8\pi}{3} - \frac{32}{9}} = \mathbf{\frac{8}{9}(3\pi - 4)}$

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Convert the Shifted Cylinder to Polar & Use Symmetry!

Substitute $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$ and $x = r\cos\theta$ into $x^2 + y^2 = 2x$:

$$r^2 = 2r\cos\theta \implies r = 2\cos\theta$$

This circle sits in the right half-plane ($x \ge 0$), swept once for $-\frac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$. Because BOTH the hemisphere $z = \sqrt{4 - r^2}$ and the disk $r \le 2\cos\theta$ are perfectly symmetric across the $x$-axis ($y \leftrightarrow -y$), we integrate over the upper half $0 \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$ and multiply by $2$:

$$V = 2 \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^{2\cos\theta} \sqrt{4 - r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta$$

Why symmetry is a lifesaver here: On $[0, \frac{\pi}{2}]$, $\sin\theta \ge 0$, so $\sqrt{\sin^2\theta} = \sin\theta$ cleanly without needing absolute value bars $|\sin\theta|$!

2
Evaluate the Inner $r$-Integral by $u$-Substitution

Notice how the Jacobian $r$ makes $u$-substitution work automatically! Let $u = 4 - r^2$, so $du = -2r\,dr \implies r\,dr = -\frac{1}{2}du$. Change the limits:

  • When $r = 0$: $u = 4$.
  • When $r = 2\cos\theta$: $u = 4 - 4\cos^2\theta = 4(1 - \cos^2\theta) = 4\sin^2\theta$.
$$\int_0^{2\cos\theta} \sqrt{4 - r^2}\,r\,dr = -\frac{1}{2} \int_4^{4\sin^2\theta} u^{1/2}\,du = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}\right]_{4\sin^2\theta}^{4} = \frac{1}{3}\left(4^{3/2} - (4\sin^2\theta)^{3/2}\right)$$

Since $\sin\theta \ge 0$ on $[0, \frac{\pi}{2}]$, $(4\sin^2\theta)^{3/2} = (2\sin\theta)^3 = 8\sin^3\theta$, giving:

$$\frac{1}{3}(8 - 8\sin^3\theta) = \frac{8}{3}(1 - \sin^3\theta)$$
3
Evaluate the Outer $\theta$-Integral Using $\sin^3\theta = (1 - \cos^2\theta)\sin\theta$
$$V = 2 \cdot \frac{8}{3} \int_0^{\pi/2} (1 - \sin^3\theta)\,d\theta = \frac{16}{3} \left(\int_0^{\pi/2} 1\,d\theta - \int_0^{\pi/2} (1 - \cos^2\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta\right)$$

For the second integral, let $w = \cos\theta$, $dw = -\sin\theta\,d\theta$ (as $\theta: 0 \to \frac{\pi}{2}$, $w: 1 \to 0$):

$$\int_0^{\pi/2} (1 - \cos^2\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta = \int_0^1 (1 - w^2)\,dw = \left[w - \frac{w^3}{3}\right]_0^1 = 1 - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$$

Substitute back to get the final volume:

$$V = \frac{16}{3}\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{2}{3}\right) = \frac{8\pi}{3} - \frac{32}{9} = \frac{8}{9}(3\pi - 4)$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: Half of the hemisphere of radius $2$ (the $x \ge 0$ side) has volume $\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{3}\pi \cdot 2^3\right) = \frac{8\pi}{3} \approx 8.38$. Our cylindrical drill bores out a core of volume $\frac{8\pi}{3} - \frac{32}{9} \approx 4.82$, leaving behind a thin outer shell of volume $\frac{32}{9} \approx 3.56$.

Core Intuition

1. What Is a Triple Integral? (Packing a Solid with Tiny Voxels)

A double integral builds a 3D solid out of vertical towers on a 2D map. A triple integral $\iiint_Q f(x,y,z)\,dV$ goes one level deeper: take a 3D solid region $Q$ and fill it completely with millions of microscopic sugar-cube boxes ("voxels") of volume $dV = dx\,dy\,dz$. At each tiny cube, evaluate a 3D scalar field $f(x,y,z)$ (such as density or temperature), multiply by the cube's volume $dV$, and sum every single cube inside $Q$.

What Does $\iiint_Q f(x,y,z)\,dV$ Measure?

  • When $f(x,y,z) = 1$: Adding up the volume $1 \cdot dV$ of every tiny cube inside $Q$ simply gives the exact 3D Volume of the solid $Q$: $$V = \iiint_Q 1\,dV$$
  • When $f(x,y,z) = \delta(x,y,z)$ (Mass Density in $\text{kg/m}^3$): Each tiny cube has mass $dm = \delta(x,y,z)\,dV$, so summing them all gives the Total Mass of the solid: $$m = \iiint_Q \delta(x,y,z)\,dV$$
  • When $f(x,y,z)$ is Temperature or Charge Density: $\iiint_Q f\,dV$ gives total thermal energy or total electric charge; dividing by $\text{Volume}(Q)$ gives the 3D average value!
The Golden Setup Method

2. The "Roof-to-Floor, Then 2D Shadow" Strategy

Trying to visualize all 6 integration bounds in 3D at the same time is a recipe for panic. Instead, never solve a 3D bound problem all at once — split it into a 1D laser shot followed by a familiar 2D shadow problem from Section 14.2!

The 2-Stage "Laser + Shadow" Algorithm (for $dz\,dy\,dx$ or $dz\,dx\,dy$)

  1. Stage 1 — Shoot a Vertical Laser Beam (Inner $z$-Bounds): Stand at an arbitrary point $(x,y)$ inside the footprint of $Q$ and fire a laser straight upward in the $+z$-direction.
    • Where the laser enters the solid is the Floor: $z = z_1(x,y)$.
    • Where the laser exits the solid is the Roof: $z = z_2(x,y)$.
  2. Stage 2 — Crush the Solid into Its 2D Shadow $R$ (Middle & Outer Bounds): Forget $z$ completely! Shine a bright lamp from $z = +\infty$ straight down and trace the flat 2D shadow $R$ on the $xy$-plane. (Usually found by setting $z_{\text{floor}} = z_{\text{roof}}$ or setting $z = 0$.) Now bound $R$ with $dy\,dx$ or $dx\,dy$ just like a regular double integral!
$$\iiint_Q f(x,y,z)\,dV = \iint_R \left[\int_{z_1(x,y)}^{z_2(x,y)} f(x,y,z)\,dz\right] dA$$

The Nesting Rule (Instant Self-Check!)

For ANY of the $3! = 6$ valid orders of integration ($dz\,dy\,dx$, $dz\,dx\,dy$, $dy\,dz\,dx$, $dy\,dx\,dz$, $dx\,dz\,dy$, $dx\,dy\,dz$), your bounds MUST obey the variable-nesting hierarchy:

  • Outer integral: Pure constants ONLY (e.g., $a \le x \le b$).
  • Middle integral: Functions of the outer variable ONLY (e.g., $g_1(x) \le y \le g_2(x)$).
  • Inner integral: Functions of the two outer variables (e.g., $z_1(x,y) \le z \le z_2(x,y)$).
3D Reordering & Physics

3. Changing the Order of Integration in 3D & Mass / Moments

Sometimes the floor or roof splits into two pieces when viewed from above, or the integrand cannot be integrated in the given order. Changing to a different 3D order simply means pointing your laser beam along the $x$-axis (shadow on the $yz$-plane) or along the $y$-axis (shadow on the $xz$-plane).

How to Rewrite a Triple Integral in a New Order

  1. Unpack all 6 bounds into inequalities: Write out the exact bounding equations of the solid $Q$ (for instance, $0 \le x \le 2$, $x^2 \le y \le 4$, $0 \le z \le 4 - y$).
  2. Project onto the plane of the NEW two outer variables: For example, if the target order is $dx\,dz\,dy$, the new outer variables are $y$ and $z$ — so find the 2D shadow of $Q$ on the $yz$-plane by eliminating $x$ (or seeing the largest range of $(y,z)$).
  3. Shoot a laser along the NEW inner variable: For $dx\,dz\,dy$, shoot an arrow parallel to the $+x$-axis to find $x_{\text{back}}(y,z) \le x \le x_{\text{front}}(y,z)$.

Mass, First Moments & Center of Mass of a 3D Solid $Q$

Let $\delta(x,y,z)$ denote the continuous density of $Q$ (note: many textbooks write $\rho(x,y,z)$, which we write as $\delta$ here so you never confuse density with spherical radius $\rho$ in Topics 09–10!):

  • Total Mass: $\displaystyle m = \iiint_Q \delta(x,y,z)\,dV$
  • First Moments About the Three Coordinate Planes (the "Missing-Letter Rule"): $$\begin{aligned} M_{yz} &= \iiint_Q x\,\delta(x,y,z)\,dV \qquad \text{(lever arm to } yz\text{-plane is } x\text{)} \\ M_{xz} &= \iiint_Q y\,\delta(x,y,z)\,dV \qquad \text{(lever arm to } xz\text{-plane is } y\text{)} \\ M_{xy} &= \iiint_Q z\,\delta(x,y,z)\,dV \qquad \text{(lever arm to } xy\text{-plane is } z\text{)} \end{aligned}$$
  • Center of Mass $(\bar{x}, \bar{y}, \bar{z})$: $$\bar{x} = \frac{M_{yz}}{m}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{M_{xz}}{m}, \qquad \bar{z} = \frac{M_{xy}}{m}$$
KFUPM Exam Traps in Section 14.6
  • Trap 1 — Trying to Integrate an Unintegrable Function Without Swapping Order: If you see $\sin(y^2)$, $e^{x^2}$, or $\cos(x^3)$ inside a triple integral (like Final 251 Q1!), do the trivial integral first (e.g., $dz$), then immediately sketch the 2D shadow and swap $dy\,dx \leftrightarrow dx\,dy$!
  • Trap 2 — Variable Leakage in Outer Bounds: A triple integral MUST evaluate to a pure number! If your outer bound still contains $x$ or $y$, your final answer will contain a variable — guaranteed wrong.
  • Trap 3 — Mixing Up $M_{xy}$ vs. $M_{yz}$: Use the Missing-Letter Rule: the moment $M_{xy}$ is missing the letter $z$, so it multiplies density by $z$ and finds $\bar{z} = M_{xy}/m$!

Worked Example 1: Iterated Triple Integral Requiring a 2D Order Swap

Problem Statement

Evaluate the triple integral:

$$\int_0^{\sqrt{\pi/2}} \int_x^{\sqrt{\pi/2}} \int_2^{12} \sin(y^2)\,dz\,dy\,dx$$
Final Answer

Final Answer: $\mathbf{5}$

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Evaluate the Inner $z$-Integral First

The integrand $\sin(y^2)$ does not depend on $z$ at all, so it acts like a constant during the $dz$ step:

$$\int_2^{12} \sin(y^2)\,dz = \sin(y^2)\Big[z\Big]_2^{12} = (12 - 2)\sin(y^2) = 10\sin(y^2)$$

The triple integral collapses to a double integral:

$$I = 10 \int_0^{\sqrt{\pi/2}} \int_x^{\sqrt{\pi/2}} \sin(y^2)\,dy\,dx$$
2
Spot the Trap & Reverse the Order of Integration ($dy\,dx \to dx\,dy$)

$\int \sin(y^2)\,dy$ has NO elementary antiderivative! We must reverse the order of $x$ and $y$. Read the current 2D region $R$:

$$0 \le x \le \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}, \qquad x \le y \le \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}$$

This is the triangle bounded by $x = 0$ (left), $y = x$ (bottom-right), and $y = \sqrt{\pi/2}$ (top). Slicing horizontally instead ($dx\,dy$), $y$ runs from $0$ to $\sqrt{\pi/2}$, and for each fixed $y$, $x$ runs from the left edge $x = 0$ to the slanted line $x = y$:

$$I = 10 \int_0^{\sqrt{\pi/2}} \int_0^y \sin(y^2)\,dx\,dy$$
3
Integrate in $x$, Then Solve in $y$ via $u$-Substitution

Inner $x$-integral brings out the magic factor of $y$ we needed for $u$-substitution!

$$\int_0^y \sin(y^2)\,dx = \Big[x\sin(y^2)\Big]_{x=0}^{x=y} = y\sin(y^2)$$

Now evaluate $I = 10 \int_0^{\sqrt{\pi/2}} y\sin(y^2)\,dy$. Let $u = y^2 \implies du = 2y\,dy \implies y\,dy = \frac{1}{2}du$. Update limits: $y = 0 \implies u = 0$, and $y = \sqrt{\pi/2} \implies u = \frac{\pi}{2}$:

$$I = 10 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin u\,du = 5\Big[-\cos u\Big]_0^{\pi/2} = 5\left(-\cos\frac{\pi}{2} - (-\cos 0)\right) = 5(0 + 1) = 5$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: The solid $Q$ is a vertical triangular prism of height $10$ (from $z = 2$ to $z = 12$) whose density $\delta(x,y,z) = \sin(y^2)$ depends only on $y$ and varies smoothly between $0$ and $1$. Multiplying cross-sectional weighted area $\frac{1}{2}$ by height $10$ gives an exact total mass of $5$.

Classic 14.6 Tetrahedron Archetype

Worked Example 2: Volume of a Tetrahedron Using a Triple Integral

Problem Statement

Use a triple integral to find the volume of the tetrahedron $Q$ bounded by the plane $2x + 3y + z = 6$ and the three coordinate planes $x = 0$, $y = 0$, and $z = 0$.

Final Answer

Final Answer: $V = \mathbf{6}$ cubic units.

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Stage 1 — Roof-to-Floor Bounds for $z$

Solve the slanted plane for $z$: $z = 6 - 2x - 3y$. In the first octant, a vertical laser enters at the $xy$-plane ($z = 0$) and exits at the slanted roof ($z = 6 - 2x - 3y$):

$$0 \le z \le 6 - 2x - 3y$$
2
Stage 2 — Find the 2D Shadow $R$ on the $xy$-Plane

Set $z = 0$ in $2x + 3y + z = 6$ to see where the roof meets the floor:

$$2x + 3y = 6 \implies y = 2 - \frac{2}{3}x$$

The $x$-intercept is $x = 3$ (when $y = 0$), and the $y$-intercept is $y = 2$ (when $x = 0$). Using vertical slices on this triangle gives $0 \le x \le 3$ and $0 \le y \le 2 - \frac{2}{3}x$:

$$V = \int_0^3 \int_0^{2 - \frac{2}{3}x} \int_0^{6 - 2x - 3y} 1\,dz\,dy\,dx$$
3
Evaluate Inner $z$ and Middle $y$ (With a Clever Algebra Trick!)

Inner $z$-integral gives $6 - 2x - 3y$. Now integrate with respect to $y$:

$$\int_0^{2 - \frac{2}{3}x} (6 - 2x - 3y)\,dy = \left[(6 - 2x)y - \frac{3}{2}y^2\right]_{y=0}^{y = 2 - \frac{2}{3}x}$$

Pro Algebra Trick: Notice that $6 - 2x = 3\left(2 - \frac{2}{3}x\right)$! Let $w = 2 - \frac{2}{3}x$. Substituting $y = w$ gives:

$$(3w)(w) - \frac{3}{2}w^2 = 3w^2 - \frac{3}{2}w^2 = \frac{3}{2}w^2 = \frac{3}{2}\left(2 - \frac{2}{3}x\right)^2$$

This avoids expanding messy quadratics completely!

4
Evaluate the Outer $x$-Integral via $u$-Substitution

Let $u = 2 - \frac{2}{3}x$, so $du = -\frac{2}{3}dx \implies dx = -\frac{3}{2}du$. Limits: $x = 0 \implies u = 2$; $x = 3 \implies u = 0$:

$$V = \int_0^3 \frac{3}{2}\left(2 - \frac{2}{3}x\right)^2 dx = \frac{3}{2} \int_2^0 u^2 \left(-\frac{3}{2}\,du\right) = \frac{9}{4} \int_0^2 u^2\,du = \frac{9}{4}\left[\frac{u^3}{3}\right]_0^2 = \frac{9}{4} \cdot \frac{8}{3} = 6$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: A tetrahedron in the first octant with axis intercepts $(a, 0, 0) = (3,0,0)$, $(0, b, 0) = (0,2,0)$, and $(0, 0, c) = (0,0,6)$ always has volume $V = \frac{1}{6}abc = \frac{1}{6}(3)(2)(6) = 6$. Always use $\frac{1}{6}abc$ to verify tetrahedron integrals on the exam!

Worked Example 3: Parabolic Cylinder Solid & Changing 3D Order of Integration

Problem Statement

Let $Q$ be the solid in the first octant bounded by the parabolic cylinder $y = x^2$ and the planes $x = 0$, $z = 0$, and $y + z = 4$. (a) Set up $\iiint_Q 1\,dV$ in the order $dz\,dy\,dx$. (b) Rewrite the integral in the order $dx\,dz\,dy$ and evaluate the volume.

Final Answer

Final Answer: $\displaystyle V = \int_0^2 \int_{x^2}^4 \int_0^{4 - y} dz\,dy\,dx = \int_0^4 \int_0^{4 - y} \int_0^{\sqrt{y}} dx\,dz\,dy = \mathbf{\frac{128}{15}}$

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Part (a) — Setup in Order $dz\,dy\,dx$ (Shadow on $xy$-Plane)
  • Roof & Floor ($z$): Slanted plane $y + z = 4 \implies z = 4 - y$ is the roof; $z = 0$ is the floor. So $0 \le z \le 4 - y$.
  • 2D Shadow on $xy$-Plane: Where $z = 0$, the plane hits $y = 4$. Together with $y = x^2$ and $x \ge 0$, the curves meet at $x^2 = 4 \implies x = 2$. Thus $0 \le x \le 2$ and $x^2 \le y \le 4$.
$$V = \int_0^2 \int_{x^2}^4 \int_0^{4 - y} 1\,dz\,dy\,dx$$
2
Part (b) — Rewrite in Order $dx\,dz\,dy$ (Shadow on $yz$-Plane)

Now our two outer variables are $y$ and $z$, and our laser shoots along the $+x$-axis!

  • New 2D Shadow on $yz$-Plane: Look at the inequalities involving only $y$ and $z$: $y \ge 0$, $z \ge 0$, and $y + z \le 4$. That is simply the right triangle in the $yz$-plane bounded by $0 \le y \le 4$ and $0 \le z \le 4 - y$.
  • New Inner Bounds ($x$-Laser): From $x \ge 0$ and $x^2 \le y$ (first octant $\implies x \ge 0$), taking the square root gives $0 \le x \le \sqrt{y}$.
$$V = \int_0^4 \int_0^{4 - y} \int_0^{\sqrt{y}} 1\,dx\,dz\,dy$$
3
Evaluate the Reordered Integral (Half the Algebra!)
$$\begin{aligned} V &= \int_0^4 \int_0^{4 - y} \sqrt{y}\,dz\,dy = \int_0^4 \sqrt{y}(4 - y)\,dy \\ &= \int_0^4 \left(4y^{1/2} - y^{3/2}\right) dy = \left[\frac{8}{3}y^{3/2} - \frac{2}{5}y^{5/2}\right]_0^4 \\ &= \frac{8}{3}(8) - \frac{2}{5}(32) = \frac{64}{3} - \frac{64}{5} = \frac{320 - 192}{15} = \frac{128}{15} \end{aligned}$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: The solid is a curved wedge whose base sits inside the parabola $y = x^2$ up to $y = 4$ and whose top is sliced diagonally down from $z = 4$ (at $y = 0$) to $z = 0$ (at $y = 4$). Notice that projecting onto the $yz$-plane turned the shadow into a simple straight-edged triangle, cutting our algebra in half!

Mass & Center of Mass Archetype

Worked Example 4: Total Mass & Vertical Center of Mass $\bar{z}$

Problem Statement

A solid wedge $Q$ in the first octant is bounded by the coordinate planes $x = 0$, $y = 0$, $z = 0$, and the planes $x = 2$ and $y + z = 2$. If its mass density is $\delta(x,y,z) = 3y$, find (a) its total mass $m$ and (b) the $z$-coordinate $\bar{z}$ of its center of mass.

Final Answer

Final Answer: Total mass $m = \mathbf{8}$, and $\bar{z} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}}$.

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Set Up the 3D Bounds of the Wedge

Roof: $z = 2 - y$. Floor: $z = 0$. Setting $z = 0$ gives $y = 2$, so the shadow on the $xy$-plane is the simple rectangle $0 \le x \le 2$, $0 \le y \le 2$.

2
Compute the Total Mass $m = \iiint_Q 3y\,dV$
$$\begin{aligned} m &= \int_0^2 \int_0^2 \int_0^{2 - y} 3y\,dz\,dy\,dx = \left(\int_0^2 dx\right) \int_0^2 3y(2 - y)\,dy \\ &= 2 \int_0^2 (6y - 3y^2)\,dy = 2\Big[3y^2 - y^3\Big]_0^2 = 2(12 - 8) = 8 \end{aligned}$$
3
Compute the First Moment $M_{xy} = \iiint_Q z\,\delta(x,y,z)\,dV$ and $\bar{z}$

By the Missing-Letter Rule, $\bar{z}$ needs $M_{xy}$ (multiply density $3y$ by $z$):

$$\begin{aligned} M_{xy} &= \int_0^2 \int_0^2 \int_0^{2 - y} 3yz\,dz\,dy\,dx = \left(\int_0^2 dx\right) \int_0^2 3y \left[\frac{z^2}{2}\right]_0^{2 - y} dy \\ &= 2 \cdot \frac{3}{2} \int_0^2 y(2 - y)^2\,dy = 3 \int_0^2 \left(4y - 4y^2 + y^3\right) dy \\ &= 3\left[2y^2 - \frac{4}{3}y^3 + \frac{1}{4}y^4\right]_0^2 = 3\left(8 - \frac{32}{3} + 4\right) = 3\left(12 - \frac{32}{3}\right) = 36 - 32 = 4 \end{aligned}$$

Divide by total mass $m = 8$:

$$\bar{z} = \frac{M_{xy}}{m} = \frac{4}{8} = \frac{1}{2}$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: Although the wedge reaches up to $z = 2$ along $y = 0$, the density $\delta = 3y$ is zero at $y = 0$ and heaviest near $y = 2$ — right where the sloped roof $z = 2 - y$ squeezes close to the floor! Because almost all the weight sits low near $z = 0$, the balance height $\bar{z} = 0.5$ is pulled well below the geometric height midpoint $1.0$.

From-Scratch Geometry

1. Cylindrical Coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$ — "Polar Coordinates + an Elevator"

Because there is no standalone lecture video dedicated strictly to cylindrical coordinates, let's build the entire system from ground zero! Cylindrical coordinates are the easiest 3D coordinate system in calculus: they are literally just 2D polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ on the floor, plus the exact same vertical height $z$ from rectangular coordinates.

The Circular Parking-Garage Mental Model

Imagine standing at the origin $(0,0,0)$ inside a giant circular parking garage and trying to give a friend directions to a point $P(x,y,z)$:

  1. $r \ge 0$ (Horizontal distance from the $z$-axis): First drop straight down from $P$ to its shadow $(x,y,0)$ on the $xy$-plane. Measure the horizontal distance from the central elevator shaft (the $z$-axis) out to that shadow: $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$.
  2. $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$ (Horizontal spin angle): Measure the counterclockwise angle from the positive $x$-axis to the ray pointing at the shadow $(x,y,0)$ — identical to 2D polar coordinates!
  3. $z \in (-\infty, \infty)$ (Vertical elevator height): Ride the elevator straight up (or down) by distance $z$. Literally unchanged from Cartesian!

Complete Cylindrical Conversion Formulas

  • Cylindrical $(r, \theta, z) \longrightarrow$ Rectangular $(x, y, z)$: $$x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad z = z$$
  • Rectangular $(x, y, z) \longrightarrow$ Cylindrical $(r, \theta, z)$: $$r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \quad (\text{so } r^2 = x^2 + y^2), \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}, \qquad z = z$$ Quadrant Warning: Never blindly press $\arctan(y/x)$! Always plot $(x,y)$ in the $xy$-plane to place $\theta$ in the right quadrant (add $\pi$ if $x < 0$; add $2\pi$ if $x > 0, y < 0$).

What Do Constant Cylindrical Coordinates Look Like in 3D?

  • $r = c$ ($c > 0$): Every point at horizontal distance $c$ from the $z$-axis — an infinite right circular cylinder of radius $c$ centered on the $z$-axis ($x^2 + y^2 = c^2$). That's why the system is named cylindrical!
  • $\theta = c$: A vertical half-plane hinged along the $z$-axis (like one door of a revolving door).
  • $z = c$: A flat horizontal plane at height $c$ parallel to the $xy$-plane.

2. Spherical Coordinates $(\rho, \theta, \phi)$ — "3D Radar / Globe Coordinates"

Now imagine standing at the exact center of planet Earth $(0,0,0)$ with a 3D laser pointer aimed at a satellite $P(x,y,z)$. Instead of using an elevator ($z$), spherical coordinates point straight at $P$ in 3D space using one straight-line distance $\rho$ and two angles $(\theta, \phi)$.

Exact Meaning & Allowed Ranges of $(\rho, \theta, \phi)$

  1. $\rho \ge 0$ (Greek "rho" — Straight 3D Distance from the Origin): The straight-line distance from $(0,0,0)$ to $P(x,y,z)$: $\rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$. Notice the crucial difference: $r$ is distance from the $z$-axis, while $\rho$ is distance from the origin!
  2. $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$ (Greek "theta" — Azimuth / Longitude in the $xy$-Plane): The EXACT SAME $\theta$ as polar and cylindrical coordinates! Drop $P$ vertically onto the $xy$-plane and measure counterclockwise from the $+x$-axis.
  3. $\phi \in [0, \pi]$ (Greek "phi" — Colatitude, Measured DOWN from the $+z$-Axis!): Start pointing straight up at the North Pole ($+z$-axis) and tilt downward toward $P$:
    • $\phi = 0$: Pointing straight up along the positive $z$-axis (North Pole).
    • $0 < \phi < \frac{\pi}{2}$: Upper half-space ($z > 0$, Northern Hemisphere).
    • $\phi = \frac{\pi}{2}$: Flat on the $xy$-plane ($z = 0$, the Equator!).
    • $\frac{\pi}{2} < \phi < \pi$: Lower half-space ($z < 0$, Southern Hemisphere).
    • $\phi = \pi$: Pointing straight down along the negative $z$-axis (South Pole).
    Why does $\phi$ stop at $\pi$ instead of $2\pi$? Because $\theta$ already spins a full $360^\circ$ ($0 \to 2\pi$) around the globe! Sweeping a half-circle from North Pole to South Pole ($\phi: 0 \to \pi$) and spinning it $360^\circ$ ($\theta: 0 \to 2\pi$) hits every single point in 3D space exactly once.

Deriving EVERY Spherical Formula from ONE Right Triangle!

You NEVER need to memorize spherical formulas blindly! Picture the vertical right triangle whose hypotenuse is $\rho$ (from origin to $P$), whose vertical leg along the $+z$-axis is $z$, and whose horizontal leg (parallel to the $xy$-plane) is the cylindrical radius $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$. Since $\phi$ is the angle between the $+z$-axis and the hypotenuse $\rho$:

  • Vertical leg (adjacent to $\phi$): $\displaystyle z = \rho\cos\phi$
  • Horizontal leg (opposite to $\phi$): $\displaystyle r = \rho\sin\phi$   (always $\ge 0$ because $\phi \in [0, \pi] \implies \sin\phi \ge 0$!)
  • Now plug $r = \rho\sin\phi$ into polar $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$: $$x = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta, \qquad y = \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta, \qquad z = \rho\cos\phi$$
  • Reverse Formulas (Rectangular $\longrightarrow$ Spherical): $$\rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x} \text{ (match quadrant of }(x,y)\text{)}, \qquad \cos\phi = \frac{z}{\rho} \implies \phi = \arccos\!\left(\frac{z}{\rho}\right)$$
  • Direct Bridge (Cylindrical $\longleftrightarrow$ Spherical): $$r = \rho\sin\phi,\quad z = \rho\cos\phi,\quad \theta = \theta \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \rho = \sqrt{r^2 + z^2},\quad \tan\phi = \frac{r}{z},\quad \theta = \theta$$

What Do Constant Spherical Coordinates Look Like in 3D?

  • $\rho = a$ ($a > 0$): A sphere of radius $a$ centered at the origin ($x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = a^2$).
  • $\theta = c$: The same vertical half-plane hinged on the $z$-axis as in cylindrical coordinates.
  • $\phi = c$ ($0 < c < \frac{\pi}{2}$): An upper half-cone opening upward around the $+z$-axis with half-angle $c$! (If $\frac{\pi}{2} < c < \pi$, it is a lower half-cone opening downward around the $-z$-axis.)

Worked Example 1: Converting a Point to Both Cylindrical & Spherical Coordinates

Problem Statement

Given the point in rectangular coordinates $(x, y, z) = (\sqrt{3},\; 1,\; 2\sqrt{3})$, find its representation in (a) cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$ and (b) spherical coordinates $(\rho, \theta, \phi)$.

Final Answer

Final Answer: (a) Cylindrical: $\mathbf{\left(2,\; \frac{\pi}{6},\; 2\sqrt{3}\right)}$  •  (b) Spherical: $\mathbf{\left(4,\; \frac{\pi}{6},\; \frac{\pi}{6}\right)}$

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Find Cylindrical Radius $r$ and Azimuth Angle $\theta$

Compute horizontal distance from the $z$-axis:

$$r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = \sqrt{(\sqrt{3})^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{3 + 1} = \sqrt{4} = 2$$

Find $\theta$ from the shadow $(x, y) = (\sqrt{3}, 1)$ (both positive $\implies$ Quadrant I):

$$\tan\theta = \frac{y}{x} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \implies \theta = \frac{\pi}{6}$$

Keeping $z = 2\sqrt{3}$ unchanged, the cylindrical coordinates are $\left(2,\; \frac{\pi}{6},\; 2\sqrt{3}\right)$.

2
Find Spherical Distance $\rho$ and Colatitude $\phi$

Compute straight-line 3D distance from the origin (using $\rho = \sqrt{r^2 + z^2}$ saves work!):

$$\rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} = \sqrt{3 + 1 + (2\sqrt{3})^2} = \sqrt{4 + 12} = \sqrt{16} = 4$$

Find $\phi \in [0, \pi]$ using $\cos\phi = \frac{z}{\rho}$:

$$\cos\phi = \frac{z}{\rho} = \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{4} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \implies \phi = \frac{\pi}{6}$$

Double-check via $\tan\phi = \frac{r}{z}$: $\tan\phi = \frac{2}{2\sqrt{3}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \implies \phi = \frac{\pi}{6}$ $\checkmark$.

3
Assemble $(\rho, \theta, \phi)$ in the Exact Textbook Order
$$(\rho, \theta, \phi) = \left(4,\; \frac{\pi}{6},\; \frac{\pi}{6}\right)$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: The point sits $4$ units away from the origin in the first octant, hovering steeply near the $+z$-axis (only $30^\circ$ tilted down from straight up!) above the $30^\circ$ ray in the $xy$-plane.

Worked Example 2: Negative-Coordinate Quadrants & Spherical-to-Rectangular

Problem Statement

(a) Convert the rectangular point $(-2,\; 2\sqrt{3},\; -4)$ into spherical coordinates $(\rho, \theta, \phi)$.
(b) Convert the spherical point $\left(6,\; \frac{5\pi}{4},\; \frac{2\pi}{3}\right)$ into cylindrical and rectangular coordinates.

Final Answer

Final Answer: (a) $\mathbf{\left(4\sqrt{2},\; \frac{2\pi}{3},\; \frac{3\pi}{4}\right)}$  •  (b) Cylindrical: $\mathbf{\left(3\sqrt{3},\; \frac{5\pi}{4},\; -3\right)}$, Rectangular: $\mathbf{\left(-\frac{3\sqrt{6}}{2},\; -\frac{3\sqrt{6}}{2},\; -3\right)}$

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Part (a) — Watch the Signs of $x$, $y$, and $z$!
  • Radius $\rho$: $\rho = \sqrt{(-2)^2 + (2\sqrt{3})^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 12 + 16} = \sqrt{32} = 4\sqrt{2}$.
  • Azimuth $\theta$: The shadow $(-2, 2\sqrt{3})$ has $x < 0$ and $y > 0$, so it lies in Quadrant II! The reference angle satisfies $\tan\alpha = \left|\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{-2}\right| = \sqrt{3} \implies \alpha = \frac{\pi}{3}$. Therefore: $$\theta = \pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{2\pi}{3}$$
  • Colatitude $\phi$: Since $z = -4 < 0$, the point lies below the $xy$-plane ($\phi$ must be obtuse, in $(\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi)$!): $$\cos\phi = \frac{z}{\rho} = \frac{-4}{4\sqrt{2}} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \implies \phi = \frac{3\pi}{4}$$

Thus $(\rho, \theta, \phi) = \left(4\sqrt{2},\; \frac{2\pi}{3},\; \frac{3\pi}{4}\right)$.

2
Part (b) — Spherical $\to$ Cylindrical $\to$ Rectangular

We are given $\rho = 6$, $\theta = \frac{5\pi}{4}$, and $\phi = \frac{2\pi}{3}$. Recall $\sin\frac{2\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $\cos\frac{2\pi}{3} = -\frac{1}{2}$.

First jump cleanly to cylindrical using our right triangle ($r = \rho\sin\phi$, $z = \rho\cos\phi$):

$$r = 6\sin\frac{2\pi}{3} = 6\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = 3\sqrt{3}, \qquad z = 6\cos\frac{2\pi}{3} = 6\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = -3$$

So cylindrical coordinates are $\left(3\sqrt{3},\; \frac{5\pi}{4},\; -3\right)$.

3
Finish Rectangular $(x, y, z)$ & Verify Distance

Since $\theta = \frac{5\pi}{4}$ is in Quadrant III, $\cos\frac{5\pi}{4} = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ and $\sin\frac{5\pi}{4} = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$:

$$x = r\cos\theta = 3\sqrt{3}\left(-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = -\frac{3\sqrt{6}}{2}, \qquad y = r\sin\theta = 3\sqrt{3}\left(-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = -\frac{3\sqrt{6}}{2}$$

Sanity Check: $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = \frac{54}{4} + \frac{54}{4} + 9 = 27 + 9 = 36 = 6^2 = \rho^2$ $\checkmark$!

Physical / Geometric Meaning: In part (b), $\phi = \frac{2\pi}{3} = 120^\circ$ tilts $30^\circ$ below the equator ($z = -3 < 0$), while $\theta = \frac{5\pi}{4} = 225^\circ$ points into Quadrant III ($x < 0, y < 0$), placing the point in the negative-all-three octant at exact radius $6$ from the origin.

Worked Example 3: Identifying 3D Surfaces from Cylindrical & Spherical Equations

Problem Statement

Convert each equation to rectangular coordinates and identify the exact 3D surface (with center/radius where applicable):

(a) $r^2 + z^2 = 4z$ (cylindrical)  •  (b) $\rho = 6\cos\phi$ (spherical)  •  (c) $\rho = 4\csc\phi$ (spherical)  •  (d) $r = 2\sec\theta$ (cylindrical)

Final Answer

Final Answer: (a) Sphere $x^2 + y^2 + (z-2)^2 = 4$;   (b) Sphere $x^2 + y^2 + (z-3)^2 = 9$;   (c) Cylinder $x^2 + y^2 = 16$;   (d) Vertical plane $x = 2$.

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Part (a): $r^2 + z^2 = 4z$ (Cylindrical)

Replace $r^2$ with $x^2 + y^2$, move $4z$ left, and complete the square in $z$:

$$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 4z = 0 \implies x^2 + y^2 + (z - 2)^2 = 4$$

This is a sphere of radius $2$ centered at $(0, 0, 2)$ (sitting on top of the $xy$-plane, tangent at the origin).

2
Part (b): $\rho = 6\cos\phi$ (Spherical — Multiply Both Sides by $\rho$!)

Whenever you see $\rho = c\cos\phi$, multiply both sides by $\rho$ to manufacture $\rho^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$ and $\rho\cos\phi = z$:

$$\rho^2 = 6\rho\cos\phi \implies x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 6z \implies x^2 + y^2 + (z - 3)^2 = 9$$

This is a shifted sphere of radius $3$ centered at $(0, 0, 3)$! (Memorize this pattern: $\rho = 2a\cos\phi \iff$ sphere of radius $a$ centered at $(0, 0, a)$!)

3
Part (c): $\rho = 4\csc\phi$ (Spherical — Convert $\csc\phi = \frac{1}{\sin\phi}$)

Multiply both sides by $\sin\phi$ to manufacture $\rho\sin\phi = r$:

$$\rho\sin\phi = 4 \implies r = 4 \implies x^2 + y^2 = 16$$

This is a right circular cylinder of radius $4$ centered along the $z$-axis.

4
Part (d): $r = 2\sec\theta$ (Cylindrical — Convert $\sec\theta = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}$)

Multiply both sides by $\cos\theta$ to manufacture $r\cos\theta = x$:

$$r\cos\theta = 2 \implies x = 2$$

This is the vertical plane $x = 2$ parallel to the $yz$-plane (since $y$ and $z$ are completely free).

Physical / Geometric Meaning: Notice the universal algebra trick across all four parts: rewrite $\sec/\csc$ in terms of $\cos/\sin$, then multiply through to build the fundamental Cartesian building blocks $r\cos\theta = x$, $r\sin\theta = y$, $\rho\sin\phi = r$, $\rho\cos\phi = z$, and $\rho^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$!

Worked Example 4: Writing Cones, Planes & Shifted Spheres in Cylindrical & Spherical Form

Problem Statement

Convert each rectangular surface equation into both cylindrical and spherical coordinates:

(a) Upper half-cone $z = \sqrt{3x^2 + 3y^2}$  •  (b) Horizontal plane $z = 5$  •  (c) Shifted sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 8z = 0$

Final Answer

Final Answer: (a) Cyl: $z = \sqrt{3}\,r$, Sph: $\mathbf{\phi = \frac{\pi}{6}}$;   (b) Cyl: $z = 5$, Sph: $\mathbf{\rho = 5\sec\phi}$;   (c) Cyl: $r^2 + z^2 = 8z$, Sph: $\mathbf{\rho = 8\cos\phi}$.

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Part (a): Steep Upper Half-Cone $z = \sqrt{3x^2 + 3y^2}$
  • Cylindrical: Factor out $3$ under the radical: $z = \sqrt{3(x^2 + y^2)} = \sqrt{3}\,r$ (with $r \ge 0$).
  • Spherical: Substitute $z = \rho\cos\phi$ and $r = \rho\sin\phi$: $$\rho\cos\phi = \sqrt{3}\,\rho\sin\phi \implies \frac{\sin\phi}{\cos\phi} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \implies \tan\phi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$$ Since $z \ge 0$, $\phi \in [0, \frac{\pi}{2}]$, giving $\phi = \frac{\pi}{6}$.
2
Part (b): Horizontal Plane $z = 5$
  • Cylindrical: $z$ stays $z$, so the equation is simply $z = 5$.
  • Spherical: Substitute $z = \rho\cos\phi$: $$\rho\cos\phi = 5 \implies \rho = \frac{5}{\cos\phi} = 5\sec\phi$$ Notice why this makes sense geometrically: Straight up at $\phi = 0$, the plane is distance $\rho = 5\sec 0 = 5$ away. As you tilt closer to horizontal ($\phi \to \frac{\pi}{2}^-$), your laser hits the plane farther and farther away ($\rho = 5\sec\phi \to +\infty$)!
3
Part (c): Shifted Sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 8z = 0$
  • Cylindrical: Replace $x^2 + y^2$ with $r^2$: $r^2 + z^2 = 8z$.
  • Spherical: Replace $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = \rho^2$ and $z = \rho\cos\phi$: $$\rho^2 = 8\rho\cos\phi \implies \rho(\rho - 8\cos\phi) = 0 \implies \rho = 8\cos\phi$$ (We may safely drop the separate factor $\rho = 0$ because the origin is already generated when $\phi = \frac{\pi}{2}$!)

Physical / Geometric Meaning: Mastering these three translations is the single key to Section 14.7 — every cone becomes a constant $\phi$-bound, every horizontal plane becomes $\rho = c\sec\phi$, and every origin-tangent sphere becomes $\rho = 2a\cos\phi$!

Deep Derivation

3. WHY Is the Spherical Volume Element $dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta$?

Students often misremember $\rho^2\sin\phi$ as $\rho\sin\phi$ or $\rho^2\cos\phi$. Spend 30 seconds tracing the three perpendicular edges of one tiny spherical block and the formula will be permanently locked into your intuition!

Measuring the 3 Perpendicular Edges of a Spherical Voxel

Fix a point at $(\rho, \theta, \phi)$ and nudge each coordinate slightly by $\Delta\rho$, $\Delta\phi$, and $\Delta\theta$. Because the three spherical coordinate directions are mutually perpendicular, the tiny carved-out wedge is approximately a rectangular box whose three edge lengths are:

  1. Edge 1 — Radial Thickness (nudging $\rho$ outward along the laser ray): Moving straight out from $\rho$ to $\rho + \Delta\rho$ travels a straight distance of: $$\text{Edge}_1 = \Delta\rho$$
  2. Edge 2 — North-to-South Meridian Arc (tilting $\phi$ by $\Delta\phi$): Tilting $\phi$ swings the tip of the laser along a vertical great circle of radius $\rho$. By arc length $s = (\text{radius})(\text{angle})$: $$\text{Edge}_2 = \rho\,\Delta\phi$$
  3. Edge 3 — East-to-West Latitude Circle Arc (spinning $\theta$ by $\Delta\theta$): Spinning $\theta$ moves the point horizontally along a circle centered on the $z$-axis! What is the radius of THAT horizontal circle? It is NOT $\rho$ — it is the cylindrical radius $r = \rho\sin\phi$! (Near the North Pole where $\phi \approx 0$, the circle is tiny because $\sin\phi \approx 0$; at the Equator $\phi = \frac{\pi}{2}$, the circle has full radius $\rho$ because $\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 1$!) Therefore: $$\text{Edge}_3 = r\,\Delta\theta = (\rho\sin\phi)\,\Delta\theta$$

Multiplying the 3 Edges $\implies$ Spherical Triple Integral Formula

$$dV = (\text{Edge}_1)(\text{Edge}_2)(\text{Edge}_3) = (d\rho)(\rho\,d\phi)(\rho\sin\phi\,d\theta) = \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta$$

Dimensional Check: $\rho^2$ ($\text{m}^2$) $\times\; d\rho$ ($\text{m}$) $\times\; \sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta$ (dimensionless) $= \text{m}^3$ (true 3D volume!).

$$\iiint_Q f(x,y,z)\,dV = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} \int_{\phi_1}^{\phi_2} \int_{\rho_1(\theta,\phi)}^{\rho_2(\theta,\phi)} f(\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\, \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\, \rho\cos\phi)\,\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta$$

  • Trap 3 — Using $\phi \in [0, \pi]$ for the Shifted Sphere $\rho = 2a\cos\phi$: The entire sphere $x^2 + y^2 + (z - a)^2 \le a^2$ lies above the $xy$-plane ($z \ge 0$), so $\phi$ MUST stop at $\frac{\pi}{2}$! Beyond $\frac{\pi}{2}$, $\cos\phi$ turns negative.

Worked Example 1: Volume of an Ice-Cream Cone in Spherical Coordinates

Problem Statement

Find the volume of the solid region bounded below by the upper nappe of the cone $z^2 = x^2 + y^2$ and above by the sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4$.

Final Answer

Final Answer: $V = \mathbf{\frac{8\pi}{3}\left(2 - \sqrt{2}\right)}$

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Translate Both Bounding Surfaces into Spherical Coordinates
  • Sphere: $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4 \implies \rho^2 = 4 \implies \rho = 2$ (since $\rho \ge 0$).
  • Upper nappe of cone: $z = +\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \implies \rho\cos\phi = \rho\sin\phi \implies \tan\phi = 1 \implies \phi = \frac{\pi}{4}$.
2
Read Off All Three Spherical Bounds
  • $\rho$: Laser starts at the cone tip (origin, $\rho = 0$) and exits at the spherical cap ($\rho = 2$) $\implies 0 \le \rho \le 2$.
  • $\phi$: Starts straight up along the $+z$-axis ($\phi = 0$) and tilts outward until it hits the cone wall ($\phi = \frac{\pi}{4}$) $\implies 0 \le \phi \le \frac{\pi}{4}$.
  • $\theta$: Complete $360^\circ$ axial symmetry $\implies 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$.
$$V = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\pi/4} \int_0^2 \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta$$
3
Factor into Three Independent 1D Integrals and Multiply!

Because all six limits are constants and the integrand factors cleanly as $(1)(\sin\phi)(\rho^2)$:

$$\begin{aligned} V &= \left(\int_0^{2\pi} 1\,d\theta\right)\left(\int_0^{\pi/4} \sin\phi\,d\phi\right)\left(\int_0^2 \rho^2\,d\rho\right) \\ &= (2\pi) \cdot \Big[-\cos\phi\Big]_0^{\pi/4} \cdot \left[\frac{\rho^3}{3}\right]_0^2 \\ &= 2\pi \cdot \left(-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} - (-1)\right) \cdot \frac{8}{3} \\ &= 2\pi \cdot \left(\frac{2 - \sqrt{2}}{2}\right) \cdot \frac{8}{3} = \frac{8\pi}{3}\left(2 - \sqrt{2}\right) \end{aligned}$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: An entire ball of radius $2$ has volume $\frac{4}{3}\pi(2^3) = \frac{32\pi}{3}$, so the upper hemisphere has volume $\frac{16\pi}{3}$. Our $45^\circ$ ice-cream cone carves out a fraction $\frac{2 - \sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 29.3\%$ of the upper hemisphere, giving $\frac{8\pi}{3}(2 - \sqrt{2}) \approx 4.91$ cubic units!

Worked Example 2: Converting a Rectangular Triple Integral Directly to Spherical

Problem Statement

Convert the following rectangular triple integral into spherical coordinates and evaluate it:

$$I = \int_0^3 \int_0^{\sqrt{9 - x^2}} \int_0^{\sqrt{9 - x^2 - y^2}} \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\,dz\,dy\,dx$$
Final Answer

Final Answer: $\displaystyle I = \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^3 \rho^3\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta = \mathbf{\frac{81\pi}{8}}$

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Decode the 3D Solid from the Rectangular Limits
  • $z$-bounds: $0 \le z \le \sqrt{9 - x^2 - y^2} \implies z \ge 0$ and $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \le 9$. So we stay inside the upper hemisphere of radius $3$ ($0 \le \rho \le 3$ and $0 \le \phi \le \frac{\pi}{2}$).
  • $x, y$-shadow: $0 \le x \le 3$ and $0 \le y \le \sqrt{9 - x^2}$. Both $x \ge 0$ and $y \ge 0$ restrict the shadow strictly to Quadrant I ($0 \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$)!

Together, $Q$ is the first-octant eighth-ball of radius $3$: $0 \le \rho \le 3$, $0 \le \phi \le \frac{\pi}{2}$, $0 \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$.

2
Convert the Integrand & Attach the Spherical Jacobian $\rho^2\sin\phi$

The integrand is $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} = \rho$, and $dz\,dy\,dx = \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta$. Multiply carefully:

$$\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\,dz\,dy\,dx = (\rho)\left(\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\right) = \rho^3\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta$$

Exam Variation Alert (Final 252 Q20): If the integrand is $(x^2 + y^2 + z^2) = \rho^2$ without the square root, multiplying by $\rho^2\sin\phi$ yields $\rho^4\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta$ instead!

3
Evaluate the Converted Integral
$$\begin{aligned} I &= \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^3 \rho^3\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta \\ &= \left(\int_0^{\pi/2} d\theta\right)\left(\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin\phi\,d\phi\right)\left(\int_0^3 \rho^3\,d\rho\right) \\ &= \left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\Big[-\cos\phi\Big]_0^{\pi/2}\left[\frac{\rho^4}{4}\right]_0^3 = \frac{\pi}{2} \cdot (0 - (-1)) \cdot \frac{81}{4} = \frac{81\pi}{8} \end{aligned}$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: Think of $Q$ as one-eighth of a sphere of radius $3$ whose density increases linearly with distance from the center ($\delta = \rho$). Dividing total mass $\frac{81\pi}{8}$ by volume $\frac{1}{8}\left(\frac{4}{3}\pi \cdot 27\right) = \frac{9\pi}{2}$ reveals an average distance from the origin of $\frac{81\pi/8}{9\pi/2} = \frac{9}{4} = 2.25$ — which is $\frac{3}{4}$ of the radius $3$ (because far more volume sits near the outer shell than near the center)!

Worked Example 3: Converting a Rectangular Paraboloid Integral to Cylindrical

Problem Statement

Evaluate the triple integral by converting to cylindrical coordinates:

$$I = \int_{-2}^2 \int_{-\sqrt{4 - x^2}}^{\sqrt{4 - x^2}} \int_{x^2 + y^2}^{4} \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\,dz\,dy\,dx$$
Final Answer

Final Answer: $I = \mathbf{\frac{128\pi}{15}}$

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Identify the Solid & Why Cylindrical Beats Spherical
  • $z$-bounds: Floor is the upward paraboloid $z = x^2 + y^2 = r^2$; roof is the flat plane $z = 4$. (A paraboloid + horizontal plane is the textbook cue for cylindrical!)
  • 2D Shadow on $xy$-plane: $-2 \le x \le 2$ and $-\sqrt{4 - x^2} \le y \le \sqrt{4 - x^2}$ is the complete circular disk $x^2 + y^2 \le 4$: $0 \le r \le 2$ and $0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$.
2
Convert the Integrand & Remember the Extra $r$

$\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = r$, and $dz\,dy\,dx = r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta$. Multiplying gives $r \cdot r = r^2$:

$$I = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^2 \int_{r^2}^4 r^2\,dz\,dr\,d\theta$$
3
Evaluate Step-by-Step ($z \to r \to \theta$)

Inner $z$-integral ($r^2$ is constant with respect to $z$):

$$\int_{r^2}^4 r^2\,dz = r^2(4 - r^2) = 4r^2 - r^4$$

Middle $r$-integral:

$$\int_0^2 (4r^2 - r^4)\,dr = \left[\frac{4}{3}r^3 - \frac{1}{5}r^5\right]_0^2 = \frac{4(8)}{3} - \frac{32}{5} = \frac{32}{3} - \frac{32}{5} = \frac{160 - 96}{15} = \frac{64}{15}$$

Outer $\theta$-integral:

$$I = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{64}{15}\,d\theta = 2\pi \cdot \frac{64}{15} = \frac{128\pi}{15}$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning: This solid is a paraboloid bowl of radius $2$ and height $4$ whose density $\delta = r$ increases linearly as you move outward from the central $z$-axis. In Cartesian coordinates this integral would require nasty nested trig substitutions; in cylindrical coordinates it reduced to integrating $4r^2 - r^4$!

Worked Example 4: Volume Inside a Shifted Sphere ($\rho = 4\cos\phi$) and Above a Cone

Problem Statement

Use spherical coordinates to find the volume of the solid $Q$ bounded below by the cone $z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ and above by the shifted sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4z$.

Final Answer

Final Answer: $V = \mathbf{8\pi}$

Step-by-Step Solution
1
Convert Both Surfaces to Spherical Coordinates
  • Shifted sphere: $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4z \implies \rho^2 = 4\rho\cos\phi \implies \rho = 4\cos\phi$. (In rectangular form, $x^2 + y^2 + (z - 2)^2 = 4$: a sphere of radius $2$ centered at $(0, 0, 2)$!)
  • Upper $45^\circ$ cone: $z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \implies \phi = \frac{\pi}{4}$.
2
Set Up the Spherical Limits

For every laser ray tilted between $\phi = 0$ (straight up through the North Pole $(0,0,4)$) and $\phi = \frac{\pi}{4}$ (the cone boundary), the ray starts at the origin ($\rho = 0$) and pierces out through the shifted sphere ($\rho = 4\cos\phi$). Notice $\rho$ depends on $\phi$, so we MUST integrate $d\rho$ first, then $d\phi$, then $d\theta$:

$$V = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\pi/4} \int_0^{4\cos\phi} \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta$$
3
Evaluate Inner $\rho$-Integral, Then $\phi$-Integral by $u$-Substitution

Inner $\rho$-integral:

$$\int_0^{4\cos\phi} \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho = \sin\phi\left[\frac{\rho^3}{3}\right]_0^{4\cos\phi} = \frac{64}{3}\cos^3\phi\sin\phi$$

Middle $\phi$-integral — let $u = \cos\phi$, $du = -\sin\phi\,d\phi$. Update limits: $\phi = 0 \implies u = 1$; $\phi = \frac{\pi}{4} \implies u = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{64}{3} \int_0^{\pi/4} \cos^3\phi\sin\phi\,d\phi &= \frac{64}{3} \int_1^{\sqrt{2}/2} u^3(-du) = \frac{64}{3} \int_{\sqrt{2}/2}^{1} u^3\,du \\ &= \frac{64}{3}\left[\frac{u^4}{4}\right]_{\sqrt{2}/2}^{1} = \frac{16}{3}\left(1 - \left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)^4\right) \\ &= \frac{16}{3}\left(1 - \frac{4}{16}\right) = \frac{16}{3}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right) = 4 \end{aligned}$$
4
Finish the Outer $\theta$-Integral & Check Geometrically!
$$V = \int_0^{2\pi} 4\,d\theta = 8\pi$$

Physical / Geometric Meaning (Mind-Blowing Sanity Check!): Where do the cone $z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ and sphere $x^2 + y^2 + (z - 2)^2 = 4$ intersect? Substituting $x^2 + y^2 = z^2$ gives $2z^2 = 4z \implies z = 2$ — the exact equator of the sphere! That means our solid is literally a top hemisphere of radius $2$ (volume $\frac{2}{3}\pi(2^3) = \frac{16\pi}{3}$) glued cleanly onto a right circular cone of radius $2$ and height $2$ (volume $\frac{1}{3}\pi(2^2)(2) = \frac{8\pi}{3}$). Their sum is $\frac{16\pi}{3} + \frac{8\pi}{3} = \frac{24\pi}{3} = 8\pi$ — confirming our spherical triple integral to 100% precision!
